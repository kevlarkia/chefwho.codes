THE HALLUCINATORY MEMOIR: Yes, I Saw That Too

Chapter 4: The Charlie Years (Or: How I Learned to Love Shag Carpeting)

The house on Maple Street existed in a perpetual state of twilight. Even at noon on the brightest summer days, the heavy curtains and wood-paneled walls absorbed light like a black hole consumes stars. Perfect for hiding, terrible for healing. I was five when we moved there—old enough to form memories, young enough to question none of them. That yellow shag carpet with the black and white checkered walls became my entire universe. A universe ruled by Charlie, my mother's boyfriend with the too-wide smile and too-soft voice. "Come here, little rabbit," he'd call from the end of the hallway, which seemed to stretch like taffy whenever I had to walk it. "Let's play a game." Games with Charlie always had shifting rules. Sometimes I'd win without knowing how. More often, I'd lose without understanding why. The punishments were the only consistent part. I remember the day with the whip most clearly—though my therapist (the possibly real Dr. Levine) suggests this memory might be a composite, a mental collage of various traumas sutured together into one coherent nightmare. It was raining. Charlie had been drinking the amber liquid that made his eyes go flat. My mother was somewhere else—she was always somewhere else when Charlie wanted to play. I knocked over his drink. The wet carpet darkened from mustard to mud. His belt came off with a sound like a snake shedding its skin. "You need to learn," he said, voice so gentle it was obscene under the circumstances. "Actions have consequences." I ran. Through the living room with its perpetually blaring television. Through the kitchen with its ever-present smell of burned coffee. Into the makeshift maze of half-unpacked moving boxes that filled the back of the house. Yellow carpet. Black and white walls. Yellow carpet. Black and white walls. A dizzying, nauseating pattern that offered no hiding place. He found me. They always find you when you're small. Memory splinters here, like glass hit with a hammer. Fragments that cut when I try to piece them together: The sound of leather against skin. My mother's voice, distant, saying his name with a question mark. The smell of whiskey and sweat. The pattern of the carpet pressing into my cheek. But here's where reality starts to smudge at the edges. Sometimes when I remember this, I see myself from above, floating near the ceiling like a balloon someone let go. Sometimes there's a dog barking (we never had a dog). Sometimes my mother comes in and stops it (she never did). Sometimes there's blood on the yellow carpet (medical records show no emergency room visits in that period). I told this story when I was seven, at school, to a teacher with kind eyes and a voice that cracked when she called the number on the card she kept in her desk. "There must be some confusion," my mother told the social worker who came to our house—mysteriously clean, mysteriously bright, with Charlie mysteriously absent. "She has such an imagination. Remember when she told everyone she owned a hot air balloon?" I did remember that. I remembered believing it too, with the same certainty I felt about Charlie and the whip and the maze-like house. We moved two months later. Charlie vanished from our lives like a magic trick—now you see the monster, now you don't. The new house had beige carpets and white walls. My mother never mentioned him again, and when I did, she'd look at me with a mixture of fear and annoyance, as if I were speaking in tongues. "That never happened," became her mantra. "You're remembering it wrong." Maybe I was. Maybe the yellow carpet was actually beige. Maybe the checkered walls were wallpaper in a different house. Maybe Charlie's punishments were just ordinary timeouts exaggerated by a child's perspective. Maybe the whole thing is a fabrication, a false memory constructed from fragments of movies and books and nightmares. Or maybe my mother's reality was as malleable as mine, bending to accommodate the needs of the moment, reshaping itself around her choices until her own history became unrecognizable. The worst part isn't not knowing what really happened. The worst part is knowing I'll never know. That my foundation—the very ground of my early years—might be built on hallucinations or lies or some toxic mixture of both. Is it any wonder I grew up questioning everything I see? When your own origins are written in vanishing ink, the whole world becomes suspect.

Chapter 5: The Prescription Cocktail (Shaken, Not Stirred)

My medicine cabinet looks like a miniature pharmacy, bottles lined up with military precision, labels facing outward so I don't confuse Tuesday's little blue pill with Thursday's slightly-different-shade-of-blue pill. The bottles whisper to each other when I close the door. I'm almost certain of it. Who needs recreational drugs when you've got a legally prescribed cornucopia of psychotropics? Nothing says "living your best life" quite like being on such high doses of Seroquel that doctors get nervous when they read your chart, eyes widening as they calculate the milligrams and then glance at my relatively small frame with poorly concealed concern. "This is... quite a substantial dose," a new psychiatrist said last year, tapping my file with his pen like it might bite him. "It takes what it takes," I replied with the practiced nonchalance of someone who's had this conversation too many times. "Anything less and the shadows get chatty." He looked at me for a long moment, trying to determine if this was metaphor or hallucination. Both, doc. Always both. The journey to this personalized chemical cocktail wasn't straightforward. There were the early misdiagnoses—depression (partly right), anxiety (definitely right), attention deficit (wrong, it's hard to focus when you're constantly checking if that shadow just moved). There were the first-line medications that did nothing, the second-line treatments that made things worse, the experimental combinations that produced effects so bizarre they don't have names. For six weeks on one particular antipsychotic, everything I ate tasted like metal. Not metaphorically—literally like licking a copper pipe. I lost twelve pounds before my psychiatrist believed me. Then there was the medication that turned all my dreams into elaborate Broadway musicals. Not necessarily unpleasant, but disconcerting to wake up with show tunes about anxiety playing on repeat in your head. "Paranoia!" (jazz hands) "It's coming to get you!" (tap dance sequence). When they finally found a combination that worked—that pushed the shadows back to the periphery and turned the voices down from a shout to a murmur—it came with its own delightful side effects: constant dry mouth, the occasional hand tremor, and a profound inability to cry. Emotions still happen; the tears just don't come. Like watching a rainstorm through soundproof glass. But when your baseline pain starts at "walking on stumps through gravel" courtesy of a fused ankle (skiing accident, age 19) and chronic back pain (courtesy of existing in a human body for decades), you need something with extra pizazz just to function. Enter the second row of bottles in my cabinet: the pain management squad. The problem with pain medication is that it works too well. Not just on physical pain, but on everything else too. The emotional rawness, the existential dread, the persistent feeling that reality itself is held together with cheap scotch tape—all of it, temporarily muffled under a warm, heavy blanket. "You have to be careful," Dr. Levine always says. "With your history." My history. Such a clinical way to refer to the two years I spent self-medicating with whatever I could get my hands on after the accident. The blackouts. The lost weekends. The morning I woke up in a bathtub in a city I'd never been to before, with no memory of how I got there and someone else's shoes on my feet. Kaiser Permanente has it all documented in my medical record: "History of alcohol and substance abuse." A permanent digital tattoo that ensures every new doctor looks at me with that specific blend of caution and judgment, even though I haven't had a drink in seven years. Getting those labels removed from your record is a special kind of bureaucratic nightmare. It requires multiple letters from current providers, urine tests, signed statements, acts of Congress, and possibly a virgin sacrifice under a full moon. I've been working on it for three years. The last administrator I spoke with actually laughed when I asked about the progress. "Those flags don't really go away," she said. "They just get buried under new notes." Like memories. Like trauma. Like everything else we try to erase but only manage to cover with a thin layer of something more acceptable. So I take my carefully monitored, precisely dosed medications under the watchful eyes of providers who never quite trust me. I check in monthly, pee in cups quarterly, and repeat the story of how I haven't had a drink since 2018 until the words lose all meaning. The irony isn't lost on me—I can't always trust what I see or hear or remember, but I'm expected to be absolutely trustworthy when it comes to handling my own treatment. The system demands a level of reliability that my own brain can't guarantee. Sometimes, on my clearest days, I wonder which is the greater hallucination: the shadows that whisper at the edges of my vision, or the belief that there's a combination of chemicals out there that can make me whole.

Chapter 6: The Sex Chapter (Which You'll Pretend You Skipped)

Let's be honest—you've been waiting for this chapter. The human brain is wired for three things: food, fear, and fucking. The first two we discuss openly; the third we pretend to skip while secretly dog-earing the page. My seasonal sexual appetite increases with the precision of a farmer's almanac. Nothing says "spring has sprung" quite like making questionable decisions on dating apps at 2 AM! And yes, before you ask, this too can be blamed on the beautiful chemical carnival happening in my brain. The hypersexuality arrives with the upswing—those weeks when the medication balance tips just slightly, when the shadows retreat further than usual, when I feel almost normal except for the electric current humming just under my skin. When sleep becomes optional and confidence becomes excessive and every idea seems brilliant, especially the bad ones. "It's a mild hypomania," Dr. Levine explained during one of our sessions, if they are indeed sessions and not elaborate conversations with myself. "Part of the cyclical nature of your condition." What she doesn't say—what they never say outright—is that it feels fucking fantastic while it's happening. Like being the most alive person in a world of sleepwalkers. Like finding the cheat code to human existence. Everything is brighter, faster, funnier. Including sex. During these upswings, my dating profile gets reactivated. My standards get adjusted—not lowered, I tell myself, just recalibrated for the circumstances. My inhibitions pack a small overnight bag and take an extended vacation. The problem isn't the casual sex itself. The problem is that hypersexuality makes terrible people seem like acceptable choices. Like Jason, who talked about his cryptocurrency investments during foreplay and later "borrowed" my credit card while I was sleeping. Or Rachel, who seemed fascinating and complex until I realized she was just regular-level unstable but happened to catch me during an upswing when instability seemed like a feature rather than a bug. Then there's the aftermath. The crash that inevitably follows. The morning I wake up and the electric feeling is gone, replaced by a leaden exhaustion and the crushing awareness of what I've done and who I've done it with. The frantic checking of messages to assess the damage. The promises to myself that next time will be different. The sex itself becomes a Rorschach test for my mental state. When I'm balanced, it's a genuine connection—sometimes awkward, sometimes transcendent, always human. When I'm hypomanic, it's performance art—elaborate, technically impressive, emotionally hollow. When I'm crashing, it's impossible—my body becomes a stranger's territory, unmapped and unwelcoming. The medication complicates things further. Certain antipsychotics are notorious libido killers, turning desire into a distant memory. Others leave the wanting intact but make satisfaction impossible to achieve—the sexual equivalent of being starving in front of a feast you can't taste. Finding the balance between mental stability and sexual function becomes yet another full-time job in the already exhausting work of staying sane. Dating with my particular set of issues adds an extra layer of complexity. When exactly do you disclose that you sometimes can't distinguish between reality and hallucination? First date seems too soon ("Hi, nice to meet you, sometimes shadows talk to me, do you like Italian food?"). But waiting too long feels dishonest, like I'm hiding a fundamental part of myself. Then there's the reaction when I do tell them. Some people run—understandable, if disappointing. Some people stay but treat me differently—suddenly explaining simple concepts or checking if I've taken my medication when I express an opinion they disagree with. And some people fetishize it—find it fascinating or exotic, like I'm some kind of manic pixie nightmare girl whose instability makes me more interesting. The rarest response—so rare I've encountered it exactly twice in my life—is simple acceptance. Not ignoring it, not fixating on it, just acknowledging it as one aspect of who I am, no more defining than my eye color or my preference for savory over sweet. Those relationships lasted longer. One ended when he got a job across the country; the other when I went through a particularly rough patch and pushed him away—another casualty of my scorched earth approach to self-preservation. Sometimes I wonder if I'm capable of sustaining a long-term relationship, or if my wiring makes that impossible. If the same brain that creates shadow people and voice chatter and memory gaps is fundamentally incompatible with the stability that partnership requires. But on my better days, I remember that everyone's brain is a mess of contradictions and glitches. Mine just happens to have more colorful malfunctions than most. And somewhere out there, someone's specific kind of broken might fit with my specific kind of broken, like two puzzle pieces from different sets that somehow connect. Until then, I'll keep riding the seasonal waves, making slightly better choices during the upswings, being slightly kinder to myself during the crashes, and continuing the endless process of getting to know the stranger who lives in my skin—in all her varying appetites and capabilities.

Chapter 7: How To Convince People You're Normal (A Step-By-Step Guide That Won't Work)

Step 1: Observe the humans carefully. Notice how they greet each other without scanning for threats. How they maintain eye contact without counting the blinks. How they don't flinch when someone moves too quickly in their peripheral vision. Practice these behaviors in private, preferably in front of a mirror that you've checked multiple times to ensure it's not one of those two-way observation mirrors. Step 2: Develop a collection of stock phrases for common social situations. "How about this weather?" works in elevators. "Just living the dream!" deflects workplace inquiries about your well-being. "I should let you go" provides a graceful exit when you feel the control slipping. Memorize these phrases like lines in a play, and deliver them with the approximate emotion that seems appropriate. Step 3: When you don't stutter, people assume you're perfectly fine! This is perhaps the most valuable discovery I've made in my decades of passing as neurotypical. Fluent speech creates an illusion of competence so powerful that people will overlook the fact that what you're saying makes no sense, or that you've been wearing the same shirt for three days, or that you're responding to a question no one asked. Step 4: Become indispensable. Work longer hours than anyone else. Take on the projects no one wants. Make yourself so valuable that your eccentricities become charming quirks rather than warning signs. "Oh, that's just how she is," they'll say, "but damn, she gets results." This strategy has the added benefit of keeping you too busy to spiral into existential dread during working hours. Step 5: Better yet, become the boss. When you're in charge, behavior that would get an employee referred to HR becomes "visionary leadership." Talking to yourself? You're "thinking out loud." Paranoid about competitors? You're "security-conscious." Obsessively reorganizing the office? You have "high standards." I built a seven-figure business during one of my most unstable periods, and all anyone said was how "driven" and "detail-oriented" I was. Step 6: Maintain a rigorous schedule. Humans love predictability. If you're always at the same coffee shop at 7:15 AM, always wear blue on Mondays, always have the same lunch, deviations from your pattern become the anomaly, not your behavior itself. "She's just very routine-oriented," they'll say, not realizing that routine is the only thread keeping you tethered to their reality. Step 7: Find a socially acceptable channel for your more unusual thoughts. Start a podcast about conspiracy theories. Write speculative fiction. Become an avant-garde artist. What might be concerning coming from your accountant is perfectly normal coming from your local conceptual artist who specializes in installations about surveillance and perception. Step 8: Keep your support system and your social circle separate. The people who know about your shadow friends and your voice companions and your memory maps should never meet the people who know you as the reliable colleague who always brings excellent snacks to meetings. This requires maintaining multiple versions of yourself, which is exhausting but necessary. Step 9: When all else fails, lean into your perceived strengths. If people think you're intelligent, they'll attribute your oddities to genius. If they think you're creative, they'll call it artistic temperament. If they think you're deeply empathic, they'll assume you're just processing the world's pain. Find out how people perceive you and use it as camouflage. Step 10: Accept that it will never be perfect. No matter how carefully you observe, how meticulously you prepare, how thoroughly you camouflage yourself, there will always be moments when the mask slips. When you respond to a voice no one else hears. When you flinch away from a shadow only you can see. When you reference an event that never happened. In those moments, you have two choices: elaborate explanation or simple acknowledgment. I've found the latter works better. "Sorry, got lost in my head for a moment there," covers a multitude of slips. Most people are too absorbed in their own internal monologues to probe deeper. The truth is, "normal" is a moving target, a social construct that shifts depending on context and company. What reads as eccentric in a bank teller is perfectly acceptable in an artist. What seems concerning in a stranger may be endearing in a friend. The goal isn't to be normal—it's to be just normal enough that people don't ask questions you don't want to answer. I'm just one unexpected ankle bump away from collapsing both physically and mentally. The fused joint that never healed properly can send me to the ground if I step wrong. The carefully balanced mind can similarly collapse if the wrong memory surfaces or the wrong shadow moves or the wrong voice speaks too loudly. But most days, most people have no idea. They see the carefully constructed facade, the practiced normalcy, the strategic eccentricities that distract from the deeper strangeness. They don't see the constant calculations, the ongoing translations between my reality and theirs, the exhaustion that comes from simultaneously inhabiting two worlds—the one they live in, and the one my brain creates. And maybe that's the biggest hallucination of all—the belief that anyone is actually "normal," that there exists some standard human experience that the rest of us are deviating from. Maybe we're all just various kinds of strange, living with our own shadows and voices and memories, performing our particular version of sanity for an audience that's too busy with their own performance to notice the flaws in ours. Or maybe that's just what I tell myself to feel better about the whole thing. Either way, it works well enough to get me through another day of passing as human in a world that's only partially visible to me.

Afterword: The Joke's On You

The real joke? While you're reading about my struggles, thinking "wow, this person has issues," you'll slowly realize you're nodding along, relating a little too much for comfort. That's right—the call is coming from inside the house. We're all just one existential crisis away from questioning everything we know. You've made it to the end of this journey through the funhouse mirrors of my mind. Perhaps you came for the entertainment value—the dark humor, the outrageous anecdotes, the voyeuristic thrill of peering into a reality more fractured than your own. Perhaps you came seeking understanding of someone in your life who experiences similar challenges. Perhaps you came because the title was intriguing and you had time to kill between more important activities. Whatever brought you here, you're different now than when you started. You've spent hours inside my perspective, seeing the world through my unreliable eyes, hearing it through my selective ears, interpreting it through my pattern-seeking brain. And if I've done my job properly, some of that perspective has seeped into yours, like water slowly changing the shape of stone. You've started to question whether your memories are as reliable as you thought. You've wondered about those moments when you could swear someone called your name in an empty house. You've considered the possibility that your perception of reality is just as constructed, just as subjective, just as full of convenient fictions as mine—just with fewer shadows and voices to challenge the narrative. The line between sanity and its alternatives isn't as clear as we pretend. It's not a wall but a permeable membrane, not a chasm but a slight change in elevation. We all have moments when perception glitches, when memory fails, when thoughts take unexpected and unwelcome turns. The difference is merely one of frequency and intensity, not of kind. So go ahead. Judge me. Analyze me. Feel superior. Just remember that while you're doing that, I'm the one brave enough to put this mess on paper, while you're the one who can't stop reading it. Who's really winning here? And the next time you see a shadow move in your peripheral vision, or hear a voice that might be your own thought or might be something else entirely, or remember something with perfect clarity only to be told it never happened—remember this book. Remember that reality is a consensus, not a constant. Remember that your brain, remarkable as it is, is just as capable of manufacturing conviction as it is of perceiving truth. The only difference between you and me might be that I know I can't always trust what I see. Can you say the same? Sincerely(ish), A Completely Different Person (Today, Anyway...or am I?)
