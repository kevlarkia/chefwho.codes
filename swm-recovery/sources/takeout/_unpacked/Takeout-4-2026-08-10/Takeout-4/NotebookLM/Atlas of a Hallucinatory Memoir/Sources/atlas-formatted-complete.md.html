THE HALLUCINATORY MEMOIR: Yes, I Saw That Too

Chapter 4: The Charlie Years (Or: How I Learned to Love Shag Carpeting)

The house on Maple Street existed in a perpetual state of twilight. Even at noon on the brightest summer days, the heavy curtains and wood-paneled walls absorbed light like a black hole consumes stars. Perfect for hiding, terrible for healing. I was five when we moved there—old enough to form memories, young enough to question none of them. That yellow shag carpet with the black and white checkered walls became my entire universe. A universe ruled by Charlie, my mother's boyfriend with the too-wide smile and too-soft voice. "Come here, little rabbit," he'd call from the end of the hallway, which seemed to stretch like taffy whenever I had to walk it. "Let's play a game." Games with Charlie always had shifting rules. Sometimes I'd win without knowing how. More often, I'd lose without understanding why. The punishments were the only consistent part. I remember the day with the whip most clearly—though my therapist (the possibly real Dr. Levine) suggests this memory might be a composite, a mental collage of various traumas sutured together into one coherent nightmare. It was raining. Charlie had been drinking the amber liquid that made his eyes go flat. My mother was somewhere else—she was always somewhere else when Charlie wanted to play. I knocked over his drink. The wet carpet darkened from mustard to mud. His belt came off with a sound like a snake shedding its skin. "You need to learn," he said, voice so gentle it was obscene under the circumstances. "Actions have consequences." I ran. Through the living room with its perpetually blaring television. Through the kitchen with its ever-present smell of burned coffee. Into the makeshift maze of half-unpacked moving boxes that filled the back of the house. Yellow carpet. Black and white walls. Yellow carpet. Black and white walls. A dizzying, nauseating pattern that offered no hiding place. He found me. They always find you when you're small. Memory splinters here, like glass hit with a hammer. Fragments that cut when I try to piece them together: The sound of leather against skin. My mother's voice, distant, saying his name with a question mark. The smell of whiskey and sweat. The pattern of the carpet pressing into my cheek. But here's where reality starts to smudge at the edges. Sometimes when I remember this, I see myself from above, floating near the ceiling like a balloon someone let go. Sometimes there's a dog barking (we never had a dog). Sometimes my mother comes in and stops it (she never did). Sometimes there's blood on the yellow carpet (medical records show no emergency room visits in that period). I told this story when I was seven, at school, to a teacher with kind eyes and a voice that cracked when she called the number on the card she kept in her desk. "There must be some confusion," my mother told the social worker who came to our house—mysteriously clean, mysteriously bright, with Charlie mysteriously absent. "She has such an imagination. Remember when she told everyone she owned a hot air balloon?" I did remember that. I remembered believing it too, with the same certainty I felt about Charlie and the whip and the maze-like house. We moved two months later. Charlie vanished from our lives like a magic trick—now you see the monster, now you don't. The new house had beige carpets and white walls. My mother never mentioned him again, and when I did, she'd look at me with a mixture of fear and annoyance, as if I were speaking in tongues. "That never happened," became her mantra. "You're remembering it wrong." Maybe I was. Maybe the yellow carpet was actually beige. Maybe the checkered walls were wallpaper in a different house. Maybe Charlie's punishments were just ordinary timeouts exaggerated by a child's perspective. Maybe the whole thing is a fabrication, a false memory constructed from fragments of movies and books and nightmares. Or maybe my mother's reality was as malleable as mine, bending to accommodate the needs of the moment, reshaping itself around her choices until her own history became unrecognizable. The worst part isn't not knowing what really happened. The worst part is knowing I'll never know. That my foundation—the very ground of my early years—might be built on hallucinations or lies or some toxic mixture of both. Is it any wonder I grew up questioning everything I see? When your own origins are written in vanishing ink, the whole world becomes suspect.

Chapter 5: The Prescription Cocktail (Shaken, Not Stirred)

My medicine cabinet looks like a miniature pharmacy, bottles lined up with military precision, labels facing outward so I don't confuse Tuesday's little blue pill with Thursday's slightly-different-shade-of-blue pill. The bottles whisper to each other when I close the door. I'm almost certain of it. Who needs recreational drugs when you've got a legally prescribed cornucopia of psychotropics? Nothing says "living your best life" quite like being on such high doses of Seroquel that doctors get nervous when they read your chart, eyes widening as they calculate the milligrams and then glance at my relatively small frame with poorly concealed concern. "This is... quite a substantial dose," a new psychiatrist said last year, tapping my file with his pen like it might bite him. "It takes what it takes," I replied with the practiced nonchalance of someone who's had this conversation too many times. "Anything less and the shadows get chatty." He looked at me for a long moment, trying to determine if this was metaphor or hallucination. Both, doc. Always both. The journey to this personalized chemical cocktail wasn't straightforward. There were the early misdiagnoses—depression (partly right), anxiety (definitely right), attention deficit (wrong, it's hard to focus when you're constantly checking if that shadow just moved). There were the first-line medications that did nothing, the second-line treatments that made things worse, the experimental combinations that produced effects so bizarre they don't have names. For six weeks on one particular antipsychotic, everything I ate tasted like metal. Not metaphorically—literally like licking a copper pipe. I lost twelve pounds before my psychiatrist believed me. Then there was the medication that turned all my dreams into elaborate Broadway musicals. Not necessarily unpleasant, but disconcerting to wake up with show tunes about anxiety playing on repeat in your head. "Paranoia!" (jazz hands) "It's coming to get you!" (tap dance sequence). When they finally found a combination that worked—that pushed the shadows back to the periphery and turned the voices down from a shout to a murmur—it came with its own delightful side effects: constant dry mouth, the occasional hand tremor, and a profound inability to cry. Emotions still happen; the tears just don't come. Like watching a rainstorm through soundproof glass. But when your baseline pain starts at "walking on stumps through gravel" courtesy of a fused ankle (skiing accident, age 19) and chronic back pain (courtesy of existing in a human body for decades), you need something with extra pizazz just to function. Enter the second row of bottles in my cabinet: the pain management squad. The problem with pain medication is that it works too well. Not just on physical pain, but on everything else too. The emotional rawness, the existential dread, the persistent feeling that reality itself is held together with cheap scotch tape—all of it, temporarily muffled under a warm, heavy blanket. "You have to be careful," Dr. Levine always says. "With your history." My history. Such a clinical way to refer to the two years I spent self-medicating with whatever I could get my hands on after the accident. The blackouts. The lost weekends. The morning I woke up in a bathtub in a city I'd never been to before, with no memory of how I got there and someone else's shoes on my feet. Kaiser Permanente has it all documented in my medical record: "History of alcohol and substance abuse." A permanent digital tattoo that ensures every new doctor looks at me with that specific blend of caution and judgment, even though I haven't had a drink in seven years. Getting those labels removed from your record is a special kind of bureaucratic nightmare. It requires multiple letters from current providers, urine tests, signed statements, acts of Congress, and possibly a virgin sacrifice under a full moon. I've been working on it for three years. The last administrator I spoke with actually laughed when I asked about the progress. "Those flags don't really go away," she said. "They just get buried under new notes." Like memories. Like trauma. Like everything else we try to erase but only manage to cover with a thin layer of something more acceptable. So I take my carefully monitored, precisely dosed medications under the watchful eyes of providers who never quite trust me. I check in monthly, pee in cups quarterly, and repeat the story of how I haven't had a drink since 2018 until the words lose all meaning. The irony isn't lost on me—I can't always trust what I see or hear or remember, but I'm expected to be absolutely trustworthy when it comes to handling my own treatment. The system demands a level of reliability that my own brain can't guarantee.
