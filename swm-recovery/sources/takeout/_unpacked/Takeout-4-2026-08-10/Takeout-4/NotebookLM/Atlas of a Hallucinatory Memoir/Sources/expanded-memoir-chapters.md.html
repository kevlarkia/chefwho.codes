Chapter 6: The Sex Chapter (Which You'll Pretend You Skipped)

Let's be real—this is the chapter you'll dog-ear while telling everyone you're reading this book "for the psychological insights." Sure you are, champ. Sure you are. My seasonal sexual appetite increases with the precision of a farmer's almanac. Nothing says "spring has sprung" quite like making questionable decisions on Flicker at 2 AM! And yes, before you ask, this too can be blamed on the beautiful chemical carnival happening in my brain. Isn't mental illness FASCINATING? Here's a fun fact they don't tell you in the psychiatrist's office: antipsychotics can absolutely destroy your libido... until they don't. It's like a pendulum swinging from "sex is an abstract concept I vaguely remember from my former life" to "I have suddenly developed the impulse control and discernment of a college freshman on spring break." There's no middle ground, no gentle slope—just a cliff you periodically fling yourself off, wondering mid-fall if this was really such a good idea. The problem with having an unreliable brain is that it makes for an equally unreliable... everything else. Including judgment about who to invite into your bed. When your baseline for what seems like a good idea is already skewed, adding sexual attraction to the mix is like trying to solve a Rubik's Cube while blindfolded and on a rollercoaster. During an earthquake. In the dark. "He seemed normal at the time," is a phrase I've uttered more often than I care to admit. But then again, what do I know about normal? My version of normal includes arguing with kitchen appliances and documenting the contents of my refrigerator like I'm collecting evidence for a future trial. My "normal meter" was manufactured by the same people who make those funhouse mirrors at carnivals—the ones that make you look like you've been stretched by medieval torture devices or compressed into a human accordion. The dating apps are particularly treacherous territory for someone like me. All those carefully curated photos and strategically worded bios. Are they being deceptive, or am I being paranoid? Is that really their dog, or did they borrow it for the photo op? Did we actually have that conversation about favorite books, or did I imagine it? Why does everyone on these apps look like they're perpetually on vacation in places with impossibly blue water? Do normal people really spend that much time backlit by perfect sunsets, or is this further evidence that I'm the only actual human in a world of carefully constructed simulations? I once spent an entire date convinced the man across from me was wearing a wire. Why? Because he kept adjusting his collar, and in my universe, that's deeply suspicious behavior rather than evidence of an ill-fitting shirt. I monitored the pattern of his collar adjustments, creating an elaborate mental spreadsheet of frequency, duration, and whether his eyes darted in particular directions immediately afterward. By dessert (which I couldn't eat because my stomach was too tight with anxiety), I had decided he was definitely transmitting information about me to... someone. The government? A research team? A rival dating app conducting field research? The possibilities were both endless and absurd. Dating Conversation Attempt #347: HIM: "So what kind of music do you like?" MY BRAIN: He's probing for information that reveals demographic patterns. Standard intelligence-gathering technique. ME: "Oh, you know, a bit of everything." Strategic vagueness HIM: "Like what specifically?" MY BRAIN: Pressing for details. Classic interrogation progression. ME: "Recently I've been into ambient electronica." A lie. I haven't listened to music in weeks because it sometimes contains coded messages. HIM: "Cool! I love Brian Eno." MY BRAIN: HE KNEW YOU WOULD SAY ELECTRONICA. THIS IS CONFIRMATION THAT YOUR SPOTIFY ACCOUNT IS BEING MONITORED. ME: Smiles woodenly while calculating distance to nearest exit The worst part is when the paranoia kicks in afterward. Did they really have a good time, or were they just being polite? Are they ghosting me, or did my text not go through? Is that actually them in the coffee shop across the street, or am I now hallucinating entire people based on brief intimate encounters? Is there a secret database where dates compare notes on the weird things I said or did? Is there a support group for people who've dated me? Certain interactions stick in my mind with perfect, torturous clarity. Like the woman who excused herself to the bathroom and never returned. Maybe that's why I keep coming back to it, despite the chaos that often precedes and follows. Maybe those moments of connection—real or imagined—are worth the inevitable confusion about whether the person next to me in bed is actually there or just another elaborate construct of my desperate, lonely mind. Chapter 7: How To Convince People You're Normal (A Step-By-Step Guide That Won't Work)

After decades of practice, I've perfected the art of appearing almost-but-not-quite normal for brief periods of time. For example, when I don't stutter, people assume I'm perfectly fine! What they don't realize is that I'm just one unexpected ankle bump away from collapsing both physically and mentally. One minute I'm discussing quarterly projections with confident authority, and the next I could be under my desk, convinced the fluorescent lights are sending coded messages about my failures directly to the CEO. I'll teach you my patented method of working 14-hour days so no one has time to notice you're the "eccentric one." Bonus tip: become the boss so your eccentricity gets rebranded as "creative genius" instead of "concerning behavior." It's amazing how "potentially experiencing a psychotic break" becomes "having an innovative brainstorming session" once you're the one signing the paychecks. Step 1: Develop an encyclopedic knowledge of what constitutes "normal" behavior. Study others like you're an anthropologist encountering a new civilization. Notice how they make eye contact but not too much eye contact. How they laugh at appropriate moments in conversations. How they don't constantly check their surroundings for signs they're being watched or followed. How they manage to grocery shop without taking 27 photos of the cereal aisle "for evidence." Keep a literal notebook if necessary—I did for years, with helpful categories like "Acceptable Topics for Elevator Conversations" (weather: yes; the surveillance capabilities of elevator cameras: emphatically no) and "Normal Reactions to Dead Birds" (brief sadness: appropriate; conducting an impromptu funeral in the park: apparently excessive). Step 2: Practice your "Totally Fine" face in the mirror. No, not that one—your eyes look too wide. That's your "I'm About To Freak Out But I'm Suppressing It" face, and people can tell the difference. Try again. Relax the jaw. Think boring thoughts. Imagine you're someone who's never had an internal dialogue with their breakfast cereal. That's closer. Now hold that expression while reciting the most disturbing intrusive thought you've had today. Can you maintain the facade? No? Keep practicing. Normal people's faces don't typically reflect the horrorscape of their thoughts. That's their superpower. Step 3: Curate your anecdotes carefully. Acceptable dinner party stories include mild work frustrations and amusing pet mishaps. Unacceptable topics include the time you were convinced your neighbor was broadcasting your thoughts via microwave signals, or how you spent three days tracking the patterns of birds outside your window because you were certain they were conducting surveillance. Save those gems for your therapist (real or imaginary). Learn transitional phrases that can quickly pivot away from accidental oversharing: "But anyway, how about that local sports team?" works wonders, even if you know absolutely nothing about sports and previously believed that "touchdowns" were a feature in synchronized swimming. Step 4: Master the art of the convincing lie. "I'm just tired" instead of "I haven't slept because the shadows are particularly active tonight." "I'm not feeling well" instead of "I'm experiencing a reality crisis and I'm not sure if this conversation is actually happening." "I have plans that evening" instead of "I might be in the middle of a paranoid episode that day and I don't want to ruin your birthday party." "I meditate regularly" instead of "I spend forty-five minutes each morning talking myself out of the belief that my reflection is plotting against me." These substitutions maintain social harmony and prevent unnecessary psychiatric interventions. Step 5: Develop a system of coping mechanisms that are socially acceptable. Taking notes in a meeting? Normal. Taking photos of your locked door to verify later that you actually locked it? Less normal. Find the intersection of "keeps me functional" and "doesn't freak people out" and set up camp there. I've found that obsessive list-making can pass as organization. Hypervigilance can be reframed as "excellent attention to detail." Paranoia about food tampering becomes "health-consciousness" if you use enough buzzwords about preservatives. It's all about the marketing. Step 6: Work like your sanity depends on it, because it kind of does. Nothing masks psychological turmoil quite like productive output. While your coworkers assume you're ambitious, you're actually just keeping the demons at bay through sheer exhaustion and the structured rhythm of tasks. When people compliment your work ethic, smile and say "thank you" instead of "it's either this or scream into the void for eight hours straight." I once completed a three-month project in two weeks not because I'm exceptionally talented, but because the alternative was sitting alone with my thoughts, and my thoughts at that particular time involved elaborate theories about the building's ventilation system being designed specifically to distribute mind-altering chemicals to my office. Productivity: it's not just for advancement opportunities! Step 7: Remember that your perception of normal is skewed, which means your perception of whether you're successfully passing as normal is also skewed. What feels like a flawless performance to you might look like barely-contained chaos to others. Accept this. Work with it. When in doubt, aim for "eccentric but harmless" rather than "perfect but suspicious." I once spent an entire week speaking exclusively in formal, textbook-perfect sentences because I thought my usual speech patterns were giving me away as "not quite right." A colleague finally asked if I was preparing for a role in a period drama. Apparently, my attempt at normality was significantly weirder than my baseline strangeness. Calibration is key, and sometimes impossible. The truth is, convincing people you're normal is an exhausting, full-time job with no benefits and no vacation days. Eventually, the mask slips. The facade cracks. You find yourself explaining to your dinner companions why you're taking photos of your food "for evidence," or asking your date if they're wearing colored contacts because their eyes seem a different shade than you remembered, and are they perhaps an impostor? You'll refer to a conversation that happened exclusively in your head, or react to a sound no one else heard, or suddenly need to leave a party because the pattern in the wallpaper started rearranging itself into accusatory messages. I've had spectacular mask failures. Like the time I was giving a presentation to potential investors and became convinced mid-sentence that they were actually evaluating me for an experimental psychological study. I stopped talking about quarterly projections and started addressing what I perceived as their "real" agenda: "I understand this is actually a test of my cognitive functioning under pressure, and I want you to know I'm aware of it but will continue the simulation as required." The room went very quiet. We did not get the investment. Or the time I was at a funeral and began laughing uncontrollably because I suddenly couldn't convince myself that the deceased was actually dead rather than elaborately faking it as part of a complex insurance fraud. No explanation makes that one better. Some burned bridges stay burned. Maybe the real victory isn't in perfecting the disguise, but in finding people who don't require you to wear it. People who see the cracks and jagged edges and choose to stay anyway. People who hand you a reality check without judgment when you need it. People who don't mind if you occasionally text them at 3 AM asking if they also remember that you went to Spain together, or if that was possibly a very detailed dream (it was always a dream; I've never been to Spain). People whose definition of "normal" is expansive enough to include your particular brand of not-normal. Or maybe that's just something I tell myself to feel better about the fact that I'll never quite get it right. Who knows? Not me—I'm still trying to figure out if the birds are spies. But between you and me, I'm pretty sure that pigeon knows exactly what it's doing with that suspiciously rhythmic pecking pattern.

Chapter 8: The Diagnosis Carousel (Or: Finding Your Label at the Mental Health Fair)

Welcome, welcome, step right up! Have you ever wanted to collect diagnostic labels like they're trading cards? Well, strap yourself in for the wildest ride at the psychiatric circus—the Diagnosis Carousel! It's the only carnival attraction where YOU become the prize! Here's how it works: You sit in a tiny office with fluorescent lights that make everyone look like they've been dead for three days. A person with impressive framed papers on the wall asks you questions for approximately 47 minutes, then hands you a shiny new label that will follow you for the rest of your fucking life. Congratulations! Today you're bipolar! Spin again next month and you might win borderline personality disorder! Everyone's a winner at the Mental Health Fair! I've been riding this carousel since I was old enough to realize that not everyone has shadows whispering by them in their periphery. Not everyone hears suggestions that they should pocket that thing they don't need while shopping. Not everyone needs to take pictures of objects to prove they actually exist. "You have an overactive imagination," they said when I was five, clutching a photo of a hot air balloon and insisting it was mine. "You have bipolar disorder," they said when I was working fourteen-hour days, creating elaborate food fantasies as a chef, and occasionally stealing shit I immediately threw away. "You have delusions with paranoid features," they said when I explained about the tracking software on my phone and the six people photographing me for driving too slowly. "You have an anxiety disorder with obsessive features," they said when they saw my apartment with its meticulously organized everything, labeled storage system, and the three separate locks I check five times each before I can sleep. "You have sensory processing issues," they said when I explained that certain fabrics make my skin feel like it's being attacked by microscopic fire ants. "You have a sleep disorder," they said when I told them about the shadow people who visit between 2 and 4 AM, neither fully dream nor fully hallucination. See how fun this is? It's like diagnostic Bingo, and I'm always one square away from a complete blackout. The real magic trick of the Diagnosis Carousel is that each time it stops, you briefly believe you've found the answer. "FINALLY!" you think, "THIS explains everything!" It's like being handed a user manual for your malfunctioning brain, and for a blissful moment, you feel understood. You feel seen. You spend hours researching this new label, joining online support groups, reading memoirs by people with the same diagnosis, highlighting passages that resonate with your experience and thinking "yes, this is me, this makes sense, I'm not alone." You practice saying the new term—testing how it feels in your mouth, how it sounds to your ears. "I have [insert diagnosis here]." Does it fit? Does it explain the shadows, the voices, the memory gaps, the inexplicable behaviors? You try it on like a new coat, walking around in it, seeing if the sleeves are the right length, if it keeps you warm enough against the chill of being fundamentally different from most people around you. But here's the punchline nobody warns you about: the diagnoses don't actually fix anything. They're just different-colored lenses to view the same broken kaleidoscope. My shadows don't vanish when someone writes "hallucinations" on a chart. My impulses don't quiet when they diagnose "impulse control issues." My paranoia doesn't apologize and leave when someone identifies it correctly. "Here's what we're dealing with," the doctors say, prescribing medications that sometimes help, sometimes hurt, and sometimes do absolutely nothing at all except give you constipation and a weird metallic taste in your mouth for six weeks. I spent three years believing I was just having extended panic attacks before someone mentioned "bipolar." Then another two years thinking everything was bipolar before "psychotic features" entered the chat. Then "trauma-related symptoms" crashed the party, and suddenly my childhood with dizzying geometric wallpaper and Alexander Scott became relevant again. It's like playing a psychiatric version of Clue: "It was Bipolar, in the Frontal Lobe, with Childhood Trauma!" Except there's no satisfaction of having solved the mystery, just more questions, more appointments, more co-pays, more medications that take six weeks to maybe work if they don't make you feel worse first. The funniest part? (And by "funniest," I mean the part that makes me laugh until I'm actually crying)—sometimes I wonder if these diagnostic sessions themselves are hallucinations. What if this whole time I've been sitting alone in empty rooms, having profound conversations with chairs, nodding seriously as the air molecules explain my condition? What if the medications I take religiously are placebos I've somehow manifested? I pay $200 an hour to pour my trauma into the ears of a potentially non-existent therapist. If that's not financial efficiency, I don't know what is. Maybe I'm writing this book for an audience that doesn't exist either. Maybe you're just another shadow whispering by in my periphery, nodding along as I explain the intricate architecture of my delusions. Or maybe—plot twist—I'm the only real thing in your hallucination. Have you considered that? Maybe you're creating elaborate diagnoses to explain away the uncomfortable truth that you're just as lost as I am. Which brings me to the crown jewel of the Diagnosis Carousel: the medication merry-go-round! For every shiny new label, there's a cornucopia of colorful pills. Each with their own theme park of side effects! Enjoy sudden weight gain, random muscle spasms, and the sexual appetite of a comatose sloth! Don't worry though—if those don't work for you, we have HUNDREDS more to try! Let me tell you about the time I grew breasts on Risperdal—yes, as an adult male, I suddenly developed tender, swollen breast tissue, a delightful side effect known as gynaecomastia that exactly zero people warned me about. Or the antidepressant that made me taste colors. Not in a fun, synesthetic way, but in an "everything tastes like blue" way that made eating profoundly unpleasant. Or the anti-anxiety medication that gave me memory gaps so profound I once "came to" in a supermarket, staring at avocados with absolutely no recollection of how I got there or what I was supposed to be doing. And every time a medication doesn't work or causes intolerable side effects, it's back to the carousel: "Well, this suggests it might not be Diagnosis A, but perhaps Diagnosis B! Let's try a different approach." When you're already riding the Diagnosis Carousel, any new behaviors get stapled to your existing file. You're not having a bad day—you're having "an episode." You're not celebrating—you're "manic." You're not sad—you're "decompensating." Your entire emotional spectrum gets medicalized, pathologized, scrutinized for evidence of your underlying condition(s). So here I am, still spinning on this gaudy carousel, collecting diagnostic confetti as I go. Some days I wonder if I should just get them all tattooed across my forehead to save everyone the trouble. Other days I fantasize about burning my medical records and starting fresh somewhere where no one knows the encyclopedia of broken things I supposedly am. But here's the real kicker—the ultimate joke of the Diagnosis Carousel: sometimes the diagnoses actually help. Sometimes putting a name to the monster under your bed makes it slightly less terrifying. Sometimes finding others with your same label makes you feel less alone. Sometimes the right combination of pills actually quiets the shadows enough that you can sleep through the night. And that tiny, fragile hope is what keeps me buying tickets for this carousel ride, season after season. Because maybe, just maybe, the next spin will be the one that finally makes sense of it all. Or maybe that's just another delusion. At this point, who can tell the difference?

Chapter 9: Family Matters (The DNA of Dysfunction)

They say the apple doesn't fall far from the tree. In my family's case, the whole fucking orchard is perched on the edge of a cliff. My mom and I are twenty years apart. "We're not very far. We're very close," I tell people, which somehow sounds more like a threat than a fact when I say it out loud. Being raised by someone who's practically your slightly-older sister creates an interesting dynamic. She was wild when I was a kid—we moved seven times in one year. Try explaining that to your third-grade teacher when she asks why your emergency contact information changes more frequently than your underwear. "My mom is just really into feng shui," I'd say, which was easier than explaining that we were perpetually either running out of money or suddenly flush enough to upgrade, riding our own private financial rollercoaster. Up and down, up and down. Looking back, I wonder if she was bipolar too. The DNA of dysfunction rarely skips a generation. She would start new jobs with manic enthusiasm, full of plans and ambitions, talking about career trajectories and five-year goals. Three months later, she'd quit in a blaze of glory over some perceived slight or unbearable working condition. "They don't appreciate me there," she'd say, or "The manager kept looking at me funny." The week after quitting, she'd be in bed until 2 PM, curtains drawn, unreachable in the depths of what I now recognize as depression. Then, like clockwork, she'd bounce back with a new job, new apartment, new boyfriend, new life philosophy. Wash, rinse, repeat. I learned early how to pack an entire household in under three hours. Which items were essential (documents, medications, photographs) and which were replaceable (furniture, appliances, toys). How to change schools mid-year without drawing too much attention. How to make friends quickly because you wouldn't have them long. How to say goodbye without saying goodbye, because sometimes we left without notice, in the middle of the night, no forwarding address. "An adventure," my mom called these midnight relocations. "A fresh start." It wasn't until I was older that I understood we were usually running from something—unpaid rent, an angry ex, debt collectors, the consequences of my mother's latest manic decision. Then there was my grandfather—my real anchor, the one I was "closer to" until he died when I was 25. He lost his son (my uncle Victor Dominic, who died in his 30s), and I was the only boy, so I "kind of made up for him... I was spoiled." Grandfather made sure I had everything I ever wanted. When I did something wrong, "he wouldn't yell at me and make my mom not yell at me because... I could do no wrong." He was the opposite of my mother's chaos—stable, predictable, permanent. His house smelled the same every time we visited: pipe tobacco and old books. He wore the same style of button-down shirts my entire childhood. He ate the same breakfast every morning: two eggs over easy, wheat toast, black coffee. When everything else in my life was shifting sand, he was bedrock. For my eighth birthday, he built me a treehouse in the enormous oak in his backyard. Not some slapped-together platform, but a real, insulated structure with windows, a door, even a small desk where I could draw or read. "Every boy needs a place of his own," he said. "Somewhere the world can't get to him." I spent hours in that treehouse, watching the world through the windows, feeling simultaneously part of it and separate from it—a sensation that would become all too familiar as my own mental health issues emerged. Sometimes I wonder if he knew, somehow, what was coming for me. If he recognized the signs because he'd seen them before, in his lost son, in my mother. If the treehouse was his way of trying to give me shelter from a storm he knew I wouldn't escape. In retrospect, this complete lack of boundaries was a spectacular setup for disaster. When you don't develop a sense of right and wrong as a kid, you spend your adult life playing ethical catch-up. This explains why my internal integrity meter is more sensitive than a Geiger counter at Chernobyl. When most people steal a pack of gum, they think, "That was wrong, I won't do it again." When I steal something, I'm convinced I've permanently corrupted my soul and should probably be exiled to a remote mountain to contemplate my moral failings for the next decade. My grandfather's unconditional love, while a blessing in many ways, didn't prepare me for a world of conditions. It didn't teach me that actions have consequences or that rules sometimes exist for good reasons. It created a cognitive dissonance that I'm still untangling: the belief that I can do no wrong existing alongside the certainty that everything I do is wrong. My father—my biological one, not my real dad—is what I affectionately call a "trust fund hillbilly." That's not an oxymoron; it's a precise diagnosis. He had money but chose to spend it on drugs rather than, oh I don't know, creating stability for his family. Drug addicts with money are a different breed from drug addicts without it. They can maintain their habits longer, destroy more lives in slow motion, and generally avoid consequences that might actually motivate change. According to family lore, my dad once gave me ketamine by "accident" when I was an infant. I put "accident" in quotes because how exactly does one accidentally give ketamine to a baby? Was he trying to measure out my formula and just happened to mix up the white powders? "Oops, is this baby formula or a dissociative anesthetic? They look so similar!" I was born in a Midwestern state, "created during harvest in a pot field," and grew up around marijuana, a notorious motorcycle club, and drugs in various stages of processing and distribution. I've seen "not just the breakdown of something but bricks and bricks of marijuana and coke. Before it was being shipped..." I was a child surrounded by criminality but somehow never quite in it—like being backstage at a very dangerous play. My earliest memory of my father is him teaching me how to test the purity of cocaine. I was five. He thought it was hilarious. "The kid's got a future in quality control," he told his friends, while I wondered why everyone was laughing and why my tongue was numb. These are the kinds of childhood memories that therapists lean forward for, pens poised, expressions carefully neutral but eyes wide with professional interest. My aunt married high up in the motorcycle club hierarchy. Everything in my extended family was extreme—no mild dysfunctions, only catastrophic ones. "Everything sad. Person died for son. There's just so many terrible things happened in my family." We didn't do small tragedies. If you're going to have trauma in my family, go big or go home. My cousin went to prison for burning down his ex-girlfriend's house—with her cat inside. My uncle lost both legs in a meth lab explosion. Another aunt had seven children by six different men, all of whom she met while working as a bail bondsman. Family reunions, on the rare occasions they happened, felt like conventions for disaster artists, each person competing to share the most outrageous update on their personal catastrophe. The irony is, among all this chaos, I was the "spoiled kid." While the adults around me were busy imploding their lives, I was learning to work hard, to be praised, to achieve. "I always worked hard. There's never been a problem. I never expected anything for nothing." Maybe this was my survival mechanism—become so useful, so productive, that they'd have to keep you around. After the Alexander Scott catastrophe came Seth Braden, "the young guy." My mom had a type—troubled men who made questionable decisions. This pattern continued until Michael William entered the picture. Michael William—my stepdad, the man I call my real dad—is the plot twist in my family story. He's the character who shows up halfway through the horror movie and somehow manages to restore a semblance of sanity to the proceedings. Michael William "changed our lives. He took the wild out of Mom." She didn't drink alcohol for like 20 years after meeting him. "Didn't do anything... Mom was good. Mom's a good mom." Michael William gave me the structure I desperately needed. "I have to have it now. I know. That's because I was fucking bipolar then... Without structure, I'm screwed." He saw the chaos in me and, instead of running from it, decided to build walls around it—not to contain me, but to protect me from myself. He was the first adult in my life to set consistent boundaries. The first to enforce consequences that were proportional and predictable. The first to demonstrate that love could exist without enabling. The first to show me that structure wasn't a prison but a foundation. Michael William created routines. Dinner at 6:30. Homework before television. Chores with checklists. A place for everything and everything in its place. To someone else, these might have felt restrictive. To me, they were lifelines thrown to a drowning child. He taught me how to manage money, how to fix things instead of replacing them, how to speak to people respectfully even when I disagreed with them. Basic life skills that had somehow been overlooked in the curriculum of drug houses and midnight relocations. When Michael William died four years ago, I lost more than a stepfather. I lost my blueprint for normalcy, my translator for a world that often makes no sense to me. I'm ashamed of myself sometimes, wondering if I lived up to what he tried to make me. Did I take the foundation he built and construct something worthwhile, or did I let the chaos creep back in, like water finding cracks in concrete? My grandpa's been gone 30 years now, and just mentioning him "brings tears to my eyes." The same with Michael William. "Love now... You love them. You always will." These losses mark time in my life more accurately than calendars or birthdays—there's Before Grandpa Died and After, Before Michael William and After Michael William. The people we lose become our personal time zones. Now my mom thinks "she's dying every day," another thing I "have to deal with." She's followed the classic trajectory of a wild young woman—gradually settling down, finding stability with Michael William, and now in her older years, drinking "too much, but oh well." As she puts it, "When you worked your whole life and you do whatever you do to help people... if you want to do that, so be it." I'm in no position to judge. "Not my demons. I can't control my own." That's the real punchline of family dynamics—we spend our adult lives either becoming our parents or desperately trying not to, and somehow manage to do both simultaneously. I have my mom's intensity, my bio-dad's addictive tendencies, my grandpa's indulgence, and Michael William's craving for structure, all swirling together in the genetic stew that created whatever the hell I am. And yet, despite this collection of hereditary time bombs, I'm still functioning. Still working, still showing up, still trying to make sense of a world that seems designed specifically to confuse me. Perhaps that's the real inheritance I got from my family—not the mental illness or the trauma or the chaotic upbringing, but the stubborn refusal to give up, even when giving up would be the most logical choice. So here's to family—the people who fuck you up an
