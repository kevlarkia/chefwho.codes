Chapter 3: The Suspicious Kindness Conundrum

There's something deeply unsettling about someone finding you charming. Marco appeared in my life on a Tuesday afternoon at the community center where I volunteer twice a month (court-mandated community service looks better when you sprinkle in some voluntary good deeds). He was arranging chairs for the anxiety support group that meets after my shift, and he smiled at me like we shared a secret. "You always leave the room better than you found it," he said, gesturing to my meticulously straightened pamphlet display. "Most people don't notice those things." I mumbled something that might have been "thanks" and hurried out, uncomfortable with being perceived so accurately by a stranger. But the next week, there he was again, this time with a coffee—black, two sugars—exactly how I like it. "Noticed your travel mug last time," he explained with a casual shrug that felt anything but casual. "Thought you might appreciate a refill." Two weeks in, and he'd already established a pattern of noticing things about me that most people miss. Three weeks in, he remembered an offhand comment I'd made about a podcast I liked. Four weeks in, we were getting dinner after our shifts, and I found myself wondering how long it would take for him to reveal his true agenda. Because here's the thing: when someone seems interested in you—genuinely interested—there are really only three possibilities. One: they want something from you. Two: they're hallucinating a version of you that doesn't exist. Or three: you're hallucinating them entirely. I've been burned by all three. So while Marco talked about his work as a graphic designer and the novel he was perpetually "almost finished with," I was cataloging inconsistencies, building evidence for the inevitable moment when reality would fracture. His eyes were almost exactly the shade of my high school boyfriend's, which felt like too much of a coincidence. He had a habit of checking his phone exactly seven minutes into any conversation—was that when he received instructions from whoever was monitoring me? And most disturbing of all, he remembered things about me that I was certain I hadn't told him. Like my sister's name. Like my preference for sitting with my back to walls. "I've just been paying attention," he said when I casually tested him about it. "You mentioned your sister when you got that call last week. And the wall thing is pretty common for people with anxiety." Reasonable explanations. Too reasonable? I wasn't sure. On our fifth dinner together, I decided to set a trap. I carefully mentioned that I hated cilantro—said it with conviction, described it as "tasting like soap," the whole performance. The truth is, I love cilantro. Put it on everything. Have been known to eat it straight from the bunch like some deranged herbivore. The next week, when he suggested a new Thai place, he added, "They're really accommodating about leaving cilantro off the dishes." My heart did a strange double beat. Confirmation. But of what, exactly? That he was paying attention? That he cared? Or that he was somehow part of an elaborate construction designed to... to what? I didn't know. But the fact that I couldn't immediately identify the threat made it more threatening. I started keeping evidence of our interactions. Saved all our text conversations. Took photos of us together that I studied later, looking for inconsistencies. Recorded snippets of our conversations on my phone, hidden in my pocket. The rational part of me knew this was crossing a line, but the rational part of me rarely gets voting rights in how I conduct my life. The recordings were the most disturbing. Listening to them alone in my apartment, I sometimes couldn't recognize my own voice. Was that really how I sounded when I talked to him? That breathless, eager tone? That uncomfortable cackle of a laugh? I sounded like I was auditioning for the role of myself and failing miserably. And then came the missed call. My phone showed a 30-second call from Marco at 2:17 AM. When I mentioned it the next day, he looked genuinely confused. "I was asleep. Maybe I sat on my phone or something?" "For exactly 30 seconds? And then hung up without leaving a message?" "I don't know, tech glitch? Why are you so worked up about this?" Why indeed. Because a missed call shouldn't feel like evidence in a murder trial. Because normal people don't immediately jump to "he's lying" instead of "phones are weird sometimes." Because I was tired of living in a castle of suspicion with moats and drawbridges and alligators named Paranoia and Trust Issues. But here's the thing about castles—once you've built them, it's hard to remember how to live anywhere else. The breaking point came on what should have been a perfectly pleasant evening. Marco had cooked dinner at his place—a small apartment with surprisingly good art and disappointingly bad lighting. Everything was fine until I went to use the bathroom and noticed something on his bookshelf that made my blood turn to slush. A copy of "The Glass Menagerie." The exact same edition that sits on my bedside table. Now, Tennessee Williams isn't exactly obscure. But this particular edition—with its distinctive blue cover and silver foil lettering—isn't common. I'd found mine at a used bookstore three years ago. When I returned to the table, I must have looked like I'd seen a ghost, because Marco immediately asked what was wrong. "Nothing," I lied. "Just remembered I forgot to feed my cat." "You don't have a cat," he said, brow furrowed. "You mentioned being allergic last month." And there it was. The moment when everything crystallized. Either he was right, and I had told him about an allergy I absolutely do have, which meant my memory was even more unreliable than I thought... or I had never mentioned it, and he knew about my allergy through some other means. Both options were terrifying. I made it through dinner on autopilot, excused myself early with a headache that wasn't entirely fake, and blocked his number the moment I got home. Deleted all our text conversations, every photo, every recording. Erased every piece of evidence that he had existed in my life. Three days later, I discovered I couldn't remember the color of his eyes. A week later, I found a receipt for two coffees in my wallet with a handwritten note on the back: "Looking forward to next Tuesday. - M" A month later, the community center coordinator asked why I'd stopped coming in on Tuesdays, mentioned that "that nice young man from the anxiety group" had been asking about me. So he was real. Or at least, parts of him were. Sometimes I wonder what would have happened if I'd stayed. If I'd been brave enough to ask the questions directly instead of setting traps. If I'd told him about the shadows and the voices and my tendency to create elaborate fictions that feel true enough to build a life around. But the thing about walking on dental floss over an abyss is that looking down—acknowledging the precariousness of your position—all but guarantees you'll fall. I've fallen enough times to know I may not survive another impact. So I changed my volunteer days to Thursdays. I take a different route home. I avoid the Thai restaurant and the coffee shop and anywhere else we went together. Love is just a mutual agreement to believe in the same hallucination. Or at least, that's what I tell myself. Because the alternative—that something real slipped through my fingers because I couldn't distinguish between danger and damage—is too heartbreaking to consider. Besides, if he was real—truly real—why didn't he try harder to find me? Real people don't just disappear at the first sign of difficulty. But then again, neither do the best hallucinations.
