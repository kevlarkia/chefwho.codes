THE HALLUCINATORY MEMOIR: Yes, I Saw That Too

Chapter 1: Welcome to My Circus (No Refunds)

Last Tuesday, I spent forty-seven minutes arguing with my toaster about whether it was spying on me. The toaster, to its credit, maintained perfect composure throughout.

Buckle up, buttercup. Your reality is a luxury I can't afford.

I live in a world where the line between what's happening and what I think is happening has the consistency of wet tissue paper. Imagine walking through life constantly playing a game of "Real or Not Real?" except the stakes aren't just embarrassment—they're your grip on existence itself. Fun, right?

Let me introduce you to my daily companions—the shadows that drift at the edges of my vision. They're not the vague, amorphous kind you might imagine. No, these have definition. Sometimes they're humanoid, sliding along walls with elongated limbs. Other times they're more like smoke with intention, curling into corners where I swear nothing was a moment before. They don't speak, exactly. It's more like they emanate meaning—usually some variation of "we're watching" or "you're not alone." How thoughtful of them.

My morning routine begins before I even open my eyes. I lie still, cataloging sounds that shouldn't be there, determining whether the weight I feel at the foot of my bed is my cat or something my brain has helpfully invented to keep things interesting. Only after this mental inventory do I risk opening my eyes to greet whatever version of reality awaits me today.

Last month, I missed three days of work because I couldn't leave my apartment. Not because I was depressed or anxious—though I have seasons tickets to those shows too—but because I became convinced that someone had installed tracking software on my phone. I dismantled the device on my kitchen table, tiny screws rolling into corners, looking for hardware that exists only in spy movies and my imagination. When I couldn't find it (obviously), the only logical conclusion was that it must be even more sophisticated than I thought.

"I don't do well without routine even if it's a bad routine," I explained to my sister after she found me still in the same position eighteen hours later, surrounded by electronic components. She'd been calling for two days. "It's still a structure."

The look she gave me wasn't pity—I could handle pity. It was the careful neutrality of someone watching a glass teeter at the edge of a table, afraid that drawing attention to it might be the thing that finally sends it crashing down.

You might be thinking this sounds exhausting. You have no idea. The most relentless marathon runner has nothing on the stamina required to constantly question every input your senses provide. By comparison, running twenty-six miles seems like a pleasant afternoon stroll.

And then there are the voices. Not the inspirational kind that tells you to follow your dreams or that you look nice in that shirt. More like the kind that whispers "you need this" when I'm staring at a display of scented candles in a store I have no business being in. The aftermath is always the same—walking out with something tucked into my jacket, heart hammering so loudly I'm certain everyone can hear it, followed by the crushing wave of shame that sends me either back to return it or to the nearest garbage can to dispose of the evidence.

Last winter, I returned a bottle of nail polish to a bewildered cashier, stammering through an explanation of how it had "accidentally" ended up in my possession. She just stared at me with the blank expression of someone who isn't paid enough to care about my moral crisis. In that moment, I couldn't tell which of us was more uncomfortable. Probably me. Definitely me.

The most committed relationship in my life is with my paranoia. It's there when I wake up and there when I go to sleep, the loyal companion that never takes a day off. "These six people are photographing you because you're driving too slow," it informed me helpfully during a perfectly ordinary drive to the grocery store last week. I found myself squinting at rearview mirrors, trying to spot the conspiracy of traffic documentarians who had nothing better to do than chronicle my cautious approach to a yellow light.

Logic tried to intervene: "That's absurd. No one cares about your driving. No one is watching you."

But paranoia just scoffed, "That's exactly what THEY want you to think."

Logic never wins these arguments. It's like bringing a calculator to a knife fight.

"They started me on the pills" after the incident at my former workplace—the one where I became convinced that the ceiling vents were pumping in gas to make everyone more compliant. The medication helped. The shadows retreated from center stage to the wings, still present but less insistent. The voices became more like suggestions than commands. But they never fully left. They're like that one relative who says they're leaving the party but then just sits in their car in the driveway for hours, engine running, lights off, waiting.

The strangest part? Sometimes when things get quiet—when the medication is working particularly well or when I've managed to exhaust myself into a state of relative calm—I feel a disorienting emptiness. Who am I without this chorus of unreality? Just another person buying groceries without questioning if the produce section is an elaborate front for government surveillance? The thought is both peaceful and terrifying.

So welcome to my circus. The clowns are terrifying but impeccably dressed, the tightrope is made of dental floss, and the safety net has more holes than my attempts at maintaining healthy relationships—but damn if it isn't the most authentic show in town. No refunds, no exchanges, but I promise you this: by the time you're done reading, you'll never look at your own "boring" reality quite the same way again.

And maybe, just maybe, you'll start paying closer attention to your toaster.

Chapter 2: The Art of Self-Sabotage (I'm a Professional)

There's something about the fluorescent lighting in convenience stores at 3 AM that makes everything seem both more real and less consequential. Last night, I stood under those humming tubes, a candy bar clutched in my sweaty palm, heartbeat like a drummer who's had too much coffee.

I didn't need the candy bar. I didn't even particularly want it. But there it was, in my hand instead of on the shelf where it belonged.

Integrity, schmeggrity! While you're busy maintaining your moral compass, I'm over here playing "Did I Just Steal That Or Did I Imagine Stealing It?" It's a fun game where nobody wins, especially me! I'll compulsively take something I don't need, throw it in the garbage later, and then spend days internally flagellating myself for being such a spectacular disaster.

When the cashier asked if I wanted a receipt, I nearly burst into tears. Of course I want a receipt. A receipt is proof that I exist in the normal world, where people exchange money for goods instead of just taking them and then hating themselves for it. A receipt is a ticket to the land of Functional Adults Who Make Good Choices.

I clutched that small strip of paper all the way home, like it was some kind of talisman against the darker impulses that seem hardwired into my circuitry.

Dr. Levine says these episodes are related to my need for control, manifestations of deeper insecurities. At least, I think that's what she says. Sometimes when I'm sitting in her office—a room with walls that might be sage green or might be pale blue depending on how the light hits them—I find myself wondering if I'm actually talking to anyone at all.

"What are you thinking about right now?" she asks, pen hovering over her notepad. Her voice is always so measured, so calm. Too calm? Do real people speak with such consistent modulation?

I study the framed credentials on her wall. They look legitimate, but what do I know about the quality of paper used for psychology degrees? Maybe I printed them myself. Maybe this whole office is an elaborate construction of my mind, a safe space I've created to talk to myself while pretending someone else is listening.

"Just processing," I lie, because how do you ask someone if they exist without sounding completely unhinged?

Last week, I tried a small experiment. I "accidentally" knocked my water bottle off the small table between our chairs. It rolled toward her feet.

"Could you grab that for me?" I asked, watching carefully.

She reached down and picked it up, cool as anything, and handed it back to me. Physical interaction. That proves something, right? Unless I'm hallucinating the sensation of the bottle being placed back in my hand. Unless this is all happening in a corner of my mind that's more elaborate than I give it credit for.

Sometimes I wonder if I should ask the receptionist on my way out, "Hey, did you just see me talking to someone in that room for the past hour?" But I never do, because I'm afraid of the answer. Plus, if Dr. Levine is a hallucination, she's one of the nicer ones. She doesn't tell me to take things that aren't mine or whisper that strangers are following me. She just says things like "that must be really hard for you" and "let's explore that further."

Honestly, as hallucinations go, she's top-tier. Five stars, would hallucinate again.

The irony doesn't escape me—paying $200 an hour to pour my trauma into the ears of a figment of my imagination. I'm essentially paying my brain to talk to itself while wearing a different outfit. If that's not financial efficiency, I don't know what is.

The real knee-slapper is that I'm making "progress" with this potentially non-existent therapist. My integrity is "shit" because I "can't control myself," but hey—at least I'm working through it with someone who may or may not have a physical form.

This tendency to sabotage myself seeps into my real relationships too. Last month, I was actually doing well—showing up on time for coffee dates, responding to texts within the same day they were sent, remembering birthdays. My friend Rachel commented on how "present" I seemed.

The next day I ghosted her completely, ignored three calls, and "lost" the birthday gift I'd bought for her son. I found it two weeks later hidden behind my washing machine, though I have no memory of putting it there. When she finally cornered me at our mutual friend's dinner party, I had no explanation to offer. "I got overwhelmed," I said, which is both true and entirely inadequate.

The look on her face—confusion melting into hurt—is now added to my mental gallery of "Reasons to Hate Myself at 2 AM When Normal People Are Sleeping."

You know what they say—the definition of insanity is doing the same thing repeatedly and expecting different results. Well, I've taken that wisdom and elevated it to an art form. Why just repeat the same mistakes when you can innovate new, increasingly creative ways to undermine yourself?

Amateur hour is stealing a pack of gum and feeling guilty. Professional self-sabotage is constructing an elaborate therapeutic relationship with a person who might exist solely in your imagination, then using their advice to make life decisions.

The masterclass move, though? Documenting all of this in a book for strangers to read, knowing full well that if the therapist is real, she'll eventually read it too. That's not burning bridges—that's nuking them from orbit.

So here I am, possibly sitting alone in an empty room every Tuesday at 4 PM, having heartfelt conversations with a chair, paying bills to a practice that might be as real as my childhood claim to hot air balloon ownership. And somehow, I still show up every week, right on time, ready to do the work.

What drives this need to undermine myself at every turn? Dr. Levine suggests it's a preemptive strike—if I destroy things first, I control the how and when of the inevitable destruction. If I expose my flaws so blazingly that they're all anyone can see, then I never have to worry about being "found out." There's a certain twisted logic to it, I suppose. A scorched earth policy applied to one's own life.

And you? You're just sitting there, smugly certain that your therapist is corporeal. How boring. How conventional. How... sane. But keep reading—by the end of this book, you might start wondering about your own reality too. That appointment you have next Tuesday? Maybe double-check if anyone else can see your therapist. Just saying.

Chapter 3: My Photographic Memory (Of Things That Didn't Happen)

The beach house was magnificent—weathered gray shingles, sprawling deck overlooking the Atlantic, floor-to-ceiling windows that filled every room with golden light. For three glorious summers, it was our family's refuge from the world.

I can describe every detail: the rope hammock that hung between two sturdy pines, the collection of mismatched mugs in the kitchen cabinet, the particular creak of the third step on the staircase leading to the second floor. I remember the smell of salt and sunscreen, the texture of sand perpetually embedded in the bathroom tiles despite daily sweeping.

I particularly loved the window seat in what we called "the lighthouse room"—a circular turret at the northeast corner of the house, where I would curl up with books during afternoon thunderstorms, watching lightning dance across the water.

It's one of my most treasured childhood memories. Vivid, detailed, emotionally resonant.

There's just one small problem: we never had a beach house.

When I mentioned it to my mother last Christmas—some nostalgic comment about missing those summers by the ocean—she looked at me with that familiar mixture of concern and confusion.

"Honey, we never had a beach house. We went to your uncle's lake cabin a few times, but it was nothing like what you're describing."

I argued with her for twenty minutes before my sister intervened, gently confirming that no, there had never been any beach house, any hammock, any lighthouse room. The family vacation spot was a modest cabin in the mountains with a view of a small lake. No ocean for hundreds of miles.

Isn't it just delightful to remember everything in pictures? Especially when half of those pictures might be elaborate hallucinations? I take photos of everything now—my apartment before I leave for work, the inside of my refrigerator, the odometer in my car—just to prove to myself later that things were how I remember them.

My camera roll is a bizarre catalog of mundane images that would make no sense to anyone else but are vital evidence in my ongoing case against my own brain. "See?" I can tell myself, looking at a photo of my living room taken that morning. "The furniture was arranged this way when you left. No one came in and moved everything two inches to the left while you were at work."

My memory has played this trick on me so many times that I've developed a system. If a memory feels particularly sharp and cinematic, with strong emotional resonance—especially from childhood—there's at least a 50% chance I fabricated it entirely.

The beach house is just one example. There's also the elaborate memory of breaking my arm falling from a horse during a sixth-grade field trip (never happened), the time our family dog saved me from drowning in a neighbor's pool (we never had a dog), and my vivid recollection of playing piano in a state competition (I've never touched a piano in my life).

These aren't simple misremembrances or exaggerations. They are complete, detailed false narratives that my brain has helpfully provided, complete with sensory details and emotional responses. They feel as real to me as the memory of what I had for breakfast this morning (which, to be fair, I should probably check my food diary to verify).

Did six people follow me while taking photos because I was driving too slowly last week? Logic says no, but the memory is crystal clear: a silver sedan with six people inside, all holding up their phones as they passed me on the highway. I can describe what they were wearing. I remember making eye contact with the woman in the passenger seat. I remember the license plate.

But I've learned not to trust these high-definition memories, especially ones that feed into my paranoia. I made myself drive the same route the next day, curious if the same car would appear. It didn't, of course. But that doesn't stop the memory from feeling authentic.

Last month, I was absolutely certain I'd paid my electric bill. I remembered sitting at my kitchen table, laptop open, clicking the payment confirmation button. I could visualize the receipt email. But when the disconnection notice came, I checked my bank statement. No payment. No confirmation email in my inbox. Just another perfect memory of something that never happened.

This is why I now have sixteen apps on my phone dedicated to tracking, documenting, and verifying my daily existence. I set reminders for things seconds after doing them ("You already fed the cat. Do not feed her again."). I take photos of locked doors, turned-off stoves, completed tasks. I make voice recordings of important conversations so I can verify what was actually said versus what I think was said.

My therapist—assuming she exists—says this system of external verification isn't necessarily unhealthy as long as it doesn't control my life. "It's an adaptive coping mechanism," she says. "You're finding ways to function despite a significant challenge."

"But don't you think it's weird that I need photographic evidence that my reality is real?" I asked her once.

She smiled that measured smile. "Is it any weirder than people documenting their entire lives on social media for validation from strangers?"

Good point, imaginary doctor. Good point.

The truly unnerving part isn't just the false memories—it's the implications. If I can't trust my memory of the beach house, what else can't I trust? How many of my formative experiences—the ones that shaped my understanding of myself and the world—are elaborate fictions? Am I actually the person I think I am, or am I built on a foundation of hallucinated experiences?

Memory is supposed to be the anchor that holds our identity steady through time. When that anchor is revealed to be made of paper mache, it's hard not to feel yourself drifting.

So I document. I photograph. I record. I create external, verifiable evidence of my existence and experiences. It's exhausting, but the alternative—living entirely within the unreliable narrative of my mind—is far more terrifying.

Last week, I found a Polaroid in my desk drawer—a snapshot of me standing on a beautiful deck overlooking the ocean, wind in my hair, teenage smile on my face. For a moment, my heart soared. Proof! The beach house was real!

Then I turned it over and saw my own handwriting: "Maine vacation, photoshopped this for creative writing class, 2008."

Even my evidence needs evidence, apparently.

Who are you going to believe—me, or me? Certainly not me—I don't even believe me.
