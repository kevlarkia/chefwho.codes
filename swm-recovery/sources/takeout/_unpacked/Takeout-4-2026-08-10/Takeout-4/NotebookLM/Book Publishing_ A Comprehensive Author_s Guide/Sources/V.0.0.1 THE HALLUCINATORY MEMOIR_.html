THE HALLUCINATORY MEMOIR: Yes, I Saw That Too

Chapter 1: Welcome to My Circus (No Refunds)

Well, well, well... Buckle up, buttercup, because my book is about to take you on the most delightful rollercoaster ride through the funhouse mirrors of my mind. You thought YOUR reality was complicated? That's adorable.

Let me introduce you to my world—where tracking software mysteriously appears on my phone (Did I put it there? Who knows! Surprise!), where shadows whisper past me in my peripherals, and where I have the extraordinary talent of hearing voices tell me I'm doing everything wrong. Jealous yet? No? How peculiar.

You see, the thing about being me is that every morning starts with the same thrilling game: "Is Today Going to Make Sense?" The odds aren't great, but hope springs eternal! I wake up, blink at the ceiling, and place my mental bets on whether the shadows that "whisper by me all the time, like in my periphery" will stay in their lane today or decide to host a block party in the center of my vision.

Remember how people told you monsters weren't real when you were a kid? Hilarious! In my world, they just learned to be more subtle. They evolved from under-the-bed dwellers to these sophisticated shadow entities that play a perpetual game of peek-a-boo at the edges of my consciousness. And the really fun part? I'm never quite sure if they're hallucinations or if I'm the only one who can see what's actually there. Spooky privilege!

My routine isn't just important—it's the rickety bridge keeping me from plunging into the abyss. "I don't do well without routine even if it's a bad routine. It's still a routine. I have some sort of structure." Isn't that inspirational? Picture it on a motivational poster: a photo of me, desperately clinging to my daily schedule while the background slowly dissolves into chaos. #MondayMotivation!

And then there's the voice chat happening in my head. No, not the inspirational kind that tells you to follow your dreams. More like the kind that suggests "you need this" when I'm in a store, leading to the delightful aftermath of either throwing stolen items in the garbage or, if I'm feeling particularly brave, the walk of shame to return them. Nothing builds character quite like explaining to a cashier why you're returning something you never paid for!

The most intimate relationship I have is with my paranoia. It's the clingy partner that never leaves, whispering sweet suspicions like "These six people are photographing you because you're driving too slow." Logic says this is impossible—who has the time, resources, or interest to track random strangers driving slightly under the speed limit? But my paranoia just scoffs at logic, "That's exactly what THEY want you to think."

I bet you're reading this thinking, "This person needs help." PLOT TWIST: I've had help! "They started me on the pills" and guess what? The shadows got quieter, but they never fully left. They're like that one relative who says they're leaving the party but then just sits in their car in the driveway for hours. Rude.

The real kicker? Sometimes I actually miss the chaos when it quiets down. Who even am I without the constant soundtrack of internal chaos? Just another boring person buying groceries without questioning if the apples are surveillance devices? No thank you! Identity crisis averted by the eventual, inevitable return of my faithful mental companions.

So welcome to my circus. The clowns are terrifying, the tightrope is made of dental floss, and the safety net has more holes than Swiss cheese—but damn if it isn't the most authentic show in town. No refunds, no exchanges, but I promise you this: by the time you're done reading, you'll never look at your own "boring" reality quite the same way again.

Chapter 2: The Art of Self-Sabotage (I'm a Professional)

Integrity, schmeggrity! While you're busy maintaining your moral compass, I'm over here playing "Did I Just Steal That Or Did I Imagine Stealing It?" It's a fun game where nobody wins, especially me! I'll compulsively take something I don't need, throw it in the garbage later, and then spend days internally flagellating myself for being such a spectacular disaster. It's efficient self-destruction—why wait for the world to ruin you when you can do it yourself?

Oh, and don't worry—I'll share detailed instructions on how to utterly destroy your self-worth over incidents so minor that normal people wouldn't even remember them. You're welcome!

But here's where it gets really juicy. Remember that therapist I've been talking about? The one nodding sympathetically while I spill my guts about tracking software and shadow people? Well, I've been attending these sessions religiously for months now, baring my soul to this compassionate professional who's helping me untangle the knots in my psyche.

Plot twist: What if she doesn't exist?

Wouldn't that be delicious ? I mean, think about it—I've been paying $200 an hour to pour my trauma into the ears of a figment of my imagination. Talk about premium self-sabotage! I'm essentially paying my brain to talk to itself while wearing a different outfit. If that's not financial efficiency, I don't know what is.

Sometimes I wonder if I should ask someone, "Hey, do you see that woman sitting in that chair taking notes about my childhood?" But I don't, because I'm afraid of the answer. Plus, if she is a hallucination, she's one of the nicer ones. She doesn't tell me to take things that aren't mine or whisper that strangers are following me. She just says things like "that must be really hard for you" and "let's explore that further." Honestly, as hallucinations go, she's top-tier. Five stars, would hallucinate again.

The real knee-slapper is that I'm making "progress" with this potentially non-existent therapist. My integrity is "shit" because I "can't control myself," but hey—at least I'm working through it with someone who may or may not have a physical form! That's what we call "growth" in the self-sabotage business.

You know what they say—the definition of insanity is doing the same thing repeatedly and expecting different results. Well, I've taken that wisdom and elevated it to an art form. Why just repeat the same mistakes when you can innovate new, increasingly creative ways to undermine yourself? Amateur hour is stealing a pack of gum and feeling guilty. Professional self-sabotage is constructing an elaborate therapeutic relationship with a person who might exist solely in your imagination, then using their advice to make life decisions.

The masterclass move, though? Documenting all of this in a book for strangers to read, knowing full well that if the therapist is real, she'll eventually read it too. Nothing says "I'm committed to my own downfall" quite like publishing your suspicions that your mental health provider is a hallucination. That's not burning bridges—that's nuking them from orbit.

So here I am, possibly sitting alone in an empty room, having heartfelt conversations with a chair, paying bills to a practice that might be as real as my hot air balloon ownership at age five. And somehow, I still show up every week, right on time, ready to do the work. If that's not dedication to the craft of self-destruction, I don't know what is.

And you? You're just sitting there, smugly certain that your therapist is corporeal. How boring. How conventional. How... sane. But keep reading—by the end of this book, you might start wondering about your own reality too. That appointment you have next Tuesday? Maybe double-check if anyone else can see your therapist. Just saying.

Chapter 8: The Diagnosis Carousel (Or: Finding Your Label at the Mental Health Fair)

Welcome, welcome, step right up! Have you ever wanted to collect diagnostic labels like they're trading cards? Well, strap yourself in for the wildest ride at the psychiatric circus—the Diagnosis Carousel! It's the only carnival attraction where you become the prize!

Here's how it works: You sit in a tiny office with fluorescent lights that make everyone look like they've been dead for three days. A person with impressive framed papers on the wall asks you questions for approximately 47 minutes, then hands you a shiny new label that will follow you for the rest of your fucking life. Congratulations! Today you're bipolar! Spin again next month and you might win borderline personality disorder! Everyone's a winner at the Mental Health Fair!

I've been riding this carousel since I was old enough to realize that not everyone has shadows whispering by them in their periphery. Not everyone hears suggestions that they should pocket that thing they don't need while shopping. Not everyone needs to take pictures of objects to prove they actually exist.

"You have an overactive imagination," they said when I was five, clutching a photo of a hot air balloon and insisting it was mine.

"You have bipolar disorder," they said when I was working fourteen-hour days, creating elaborate food fantasies as a chef, and occasionally stealing shit I immediately threw away.

"You have delusions with paranoid features," they said when I explained about the tracking software on my phone and the six people photographing me for driving too slowly.

See how fun this is? It's like diagnostic Bingo, and I'm always one square away from a complete blackout.

The real magic trick of the Diagnosis Carousel is that each time it stops, you briefly believe you've found the answer. "FINALLY!" you think, "THIS explains everything!" It's like being handed a user manual for your malfunctioning brain, and for a blissful moment, you feel understood. You feel seen .

But here's the punchline nobody warns you about: the diagnoses don't actually fix anything. They're just different-colored lenses to view the same broken kaleidoscope. My shadows don't vanish when someone writes "hallucinations" on a chart. My impulses don't quiet when they diagnose "impulse control issues." My paranoia doesn't apologize and leave when someone identifies it correctly.

I spent three years believing I was just having extended panic attacks before someone mentioned "bipolar." Then another two years thinking everything was bipolar before "psychotic features" entered the chat. Then "trauma-related symptoms" crashed the party, and suddenly my childhood with yellow shag carpeting and Charlie the Wife-Beater became relevant again.

The funniest part? (And by "funniest," I mean the part that makes me laugh until I'm actually crying)—sometimes I wonder if these diagnostic sessions themselves are hallucinations.

What if this whole time I've been sitting alone in empty rooms, having profound conversations with chairs, nodding seriously as the air molecules explain my condition? What if the medications I take religiously are placebos I've somehow manifested? I pay $200 an hour to pour my trauma into the ears of a potentially non-existent therapist. If that's not financial efficiency, I don't know what is.

Maybe I'm writing this book for an audience that doesn't exist either. Maybe you're just another shadow whispering by in my periphery, nodding along as I explain the intricate architecture of my delusions.

Or maybe—plot twist—I'm the only real thing in your hallucination. Have you considered that? Maybe you're creating elaborate diagnoses to explain away the uncomfortable truth that you're just as lost as I am.

Which brings me to the crown jewel of the Diagnosis Carousel: the medication merry-go-round! For every shiny new label, there's a cornucopia of colorful pills. Each with their own theme park of side effects! Enjoy sudden weight gain, random muscle spasms, and the sexual appetite of a comatose sloth! Don't worry though—if those don't work for you, we have HUNDREDS more to try!

I've been on such high doses of Seroquel that doctors get nervous. When they started me on "the pills," the shadows got quieter, but they never fully left. They're like that relative who says they're leaving the party but then sits in their car in the driveway for hours. Rude.

At first, they gave me 600 milligrams in the hospital, then backed me down to 100 milligrams, which made everything "really bad now... almost worse than it was when I was in the hospital." Then they bumped me back up again. It's like playing pharmacological ping-pong with my neurotransmitters.

"Any cocktail for these things can take years," I read somewhere. No one can take a test and say, "Oh, this is the best thing for this guy's brain that's doing this, right?" We're not quite there yet. Maybe someday.

The medication dance is especially delightful when you have a fused ankle and chronic back pain. My baseline pain level is "walking on stumps through gravel." That's just my normal. So when you take drugs, you take too many. When you drink alcohol, you drink too much. Because you're starting from a pain threshold that's already in the stratosphere.

And that's how I ended up with "alcoholic" and "drug addict" in my medical records, labels I spent years fighting to remove. I had to pull my entire medical history, go through every AI-assisted file for the last ten years, to prove there wasn't enough evidence to support those claims. "I got drunk. It doesn't make me an alcoholic. I went out. I used some drugs." Doesn't everyone, at some point?

The difference is, when you're already riding the Diagnosis Carousel, any new behaviors get stapled to your existing file. You're not having a bad day—you're having "an episode." You're not celebrating—you're "manic." You're not sad—you're "decompensating."

Once, a new psychiatrist read my file before meeting me and asked, "How are your delusions today?" not "How are you today?" When the doctor is tracking your hallucinations before tracking your humanity, you know you've earned your platinum membership card at the Mental Health Fair.

So here I am, still spinning on this gaudy carousel, collecting diagnostic confetti as I go. Some days I wonder if I should just get them all tattooed across my forehead to save everyone the trouble. Other days I fantasize about burning my medical records and starting fresh somewhere where no one knows the encyclopedia of broken things I supposedly am.

But here's the real kicker—the ultimate joke of the Diagnosis Carousel: sometimes the diagnoses actually help. Sometimes putting a name to the monster under your bed makes it slightly less terrifying. Sometimes finding others with your same label makes you feel less alone. Sometimes the right combination of pills actually quiets the shadows enough that you can sleep through the night.

And that tiny, fragile hope is what keeps me buying tickets for this carousel ride, season after season. Because maybe, just maybe, the next spin will be the one that finally makes sense of it all.

Or maybe that's just another delusion. At this point, who can tell the difference?

Chapter 9: Family Matters (The DNA of Dysfunction)

They say the apple doesn't fall far from the tree. In my family's case, the whole fucking orchard is perched on the edge of a cliff.

My mom and I are twenty years apart. "We're not very far. We're very close," I tell people, which somehow sounds more like a threat than a fact when I say it out loud. Being raised by someone who's practically your slightly-older sister creates an interesting dynamic. She was wild when I was a kid—we moved seven times in one year. Try explaining that to your third-grade teacher when she asks why your emergency contact information changes more frequently than your underwear.

"My mom is just really into feng shui," I'd say, which was easier than explaining that we were perpetually either running out of money or suddenly flush enough to upgrade, riding our own private financial rollercoaster. Up and down, up and down. Looking back, I wonder if she was bipolar too. The DNA of dysfunction rarely skips a generation.

Then there was my grandmother—my real anchor, the one I was "closer to" until she died when I was 25. She lost her son (my uncle Buchi, who died in his 30s), and I was the only boy, so I "kind of made up for her... I was spoiled." Grandma made sure I had everything I ever wanted. When I did something wrong, "she wouldn't yell at me and make my mom not yell at me because... I could do no wrong."

In retrospect, this complete lack of boundaries was a spectacular setup for disaster. When you don't develop a sense of right and wrong as a kid, you spend your adult life playing ethical catch-up. This explains why my internal integrity meter is more sensitive than a Geiger counter at Chernobyl. When most people steal a pack of gum, they think, "That was wrong, I won't do it again." When I steal something, I'm convinced I've permanently corrupted my soul and should probably be exiled to a remote mountain to contemplate my moral failings for the next decade.

My father—my biological one, not my real dad—is what I affectionately call a "trust fund hillbilly." That's not an oxymoron; it's a precise diagnosis. He had money but chose to spend it on drugs rather than, oh I don't know, creating stability for his family. Drug addicts with money are a different breed from drug addicts without it. They can maintain their habits longer, destroy more lives in slow motion, and generally avoid consequences that might actually motivate change.

According to family lore, my dad once gave me ketamine by "accident" when I was an infant. I put "accident" in quotes because how exactly does one accidentally give ketamine to a baby? Was he trying to measure out my formula and just happened to mix up the white powders? "Oops, is this baby formula or a dissociative anesthetic? They look so similar!"

I was born in Ohio, "created during harvest in a pot field," and grew up around marijuana, Hells Angels, and drugs in various stages of processing and distribution. I've seen "not just the breakdown of something but bricks and bricks of marijuana and coke. Before it was being shipped..." I was a child surrounded by criminality but somehow never quite in it—like being backstage at a very dangerous play.

My aunt married high up in the Hells Angels hierarchy. Everything in my extended family was extreme—no mild dysfunctions, only catastrophic ones. "Everything sad. Person died for son. There's just so many terrible things happened in my family." We didn't do small tragedies. If you're going to have trauma in my family, go big or go home.

The irony is, among all this chaos, I was the "spoiled kid." While the adults around me were busy imploding their lives, I was learning to work hard, to be praised, to achieve. "I always worked hard. There's never been a problem. I never expected anything for nothing." Maybe this was my survival mechanism—become so useful, so productive, that they'd have to keep you around.

And then there was Charlie, my mom's husband after the divorce from my bio-dad. Charlie's chapter in our family story was short but left scars deep enough to warrant his own special section in this memoir. The "yellow shag carpet and black and white checkered walls" of that house are seared into my memory, along with the sounds of my mother being beaten with a whip, and me running through that maze-like house, trying to... what? Save her? Hide? I don't even know anymore.

The story goes that my mom finally left when Charlie tried to hit me. He then burned all her belongings in front of the house, a parting gift of destruction that perfectly bookended their relationship. Charlie taught me that love and violence could coexist, that homes could be both shelters and battlegrounds, and that adults who were supposed to protect you could become the very dangers you needed protection from.

After the Charlie catastrophe came Mark, "the young guy." My mom had a type—troubled men who made questionable decisions. This pattern continued until Harry entered the picture.

Harry—my stepdad, the man I call my real dad—is the plot twist in my family story. He's the character who shows up halfway through the horror movie and somehow manages to restore a semblance of sanity to the proceedings. Harry "changed our lives. He took the wild out of Mom." She didn't drink alcohol for like 20 years after meeting him. "Didn't do anything... Mom was good. Mom's a good mom."

Harry gave me the structure I desperately needed. "I have to have it now. I know. That's because I was fucking bipolar then... Without structure, I'm screwed." He saw the chaos in me and, instead of running from it, decided to build walls around it—not to contain me, but to protect me from myself.

When Harry died four years ago, I lost more than a stepfather. I lost my blueprint for normalcy, my translator for a world that often makes no sense to me. I'm ashamed of myself sometimes, wondering if I lived up to what he tried to make me. Did I take the foundation he built and construct something worthwhile, or did I let the chaos creep back in, like water finding cracks in concrete?

My grandma's been gone 30 years now, and just mentioning her "brings tears to my eyes." The same with Harry. "Love now... You love them. You always will." These losses mark time in my life more accurately than calendars or birthdays—there's Before Grandma Died and After, Before Harry and After Harry. The people we lose become our personal time zones.

Now my mom thinks "she's dying every day," another thing I "have to deal with." She's followed the classic trajectory of a wild young woman—gradually settling down, finding stability with Harry, and now in her older years, drinking "too much, but oh well." As she puts it, "When you worked your whole life and you do whatever you do to help people... if you want to do that, so be it." I'm in no position to judge. "Not my demons. I can't control my own."

That's the real punchline of family dynamics—we spend our adult lives either becoming our parents or desperately trying not to, and somehow manage to do both simultaneously. I have my mom's intensity, my bio-dad's addictive tendencies, my grandma's indulgence, and Harry's craving for structure, all swirling together in the genetic stew that created whatever the hell I am.

And yet, despite this collection of hereditary time bombs, I'm still functioning. Still working, still showing up, still trying to make sense of a world that seems designed specifically to confuse me. Perhaps that's the real inheritance I got from my family—not the mental illness or the trauma or the chaotic upbringing, but the stubborn refusal to give up, even when giving up would be the most logical choice.

So here's to family—the people who fuck you up and the people who save you, sometimes the exact same people on different days. They give you your issues, then judge you for having them. They break you, then wonder why you're broken. They love you in ways so imperfect that you spend a lifetime trying to decode whether it was love at all.

And the cosmic joke? Despite everything, despite the dysfunction and the drama and the damage, I still miss them when they're gone. That's the DNA of dysfunction for you—it binds you to people in ways that defective logic never could explain.

Chapter 10: The Memory Game (True or False Edition)

Here's a fun party trick: tell someone you have a photographic memory, and watch their eyes light up like you've just announced you can fly. "Really?" they'll ask, suddenly imagining you as some kind of superhuman freak who can memorize entire phonebooks with a glance.

"Quick! What was I wearing when we met three years ago?" "Tell me what page 47 of Moby Dick says!" "Can you remember everything ?"

I always want to answer, "Yes, absolutely everything—especially the shit I desperately wish I could forget."

My visual memory—or as the professionals so cleverly labeled it, my "visual visual visual photographic memory"—means I see everything and remember everything in pictures. Sounds like a superpower, doesn't it? Marvel Studios, call me.

Except this particular superpower comes with the kind of devastating side effect they'd put in tiny print on a medication warning label: May cause subject to question entire reality and develop elaborate systems to differentiate between actual events and hallucinations.

See, when your memory works in high-definition pictures and your brain occasionally manufactures its own Hollywood productions, telling fact from fiction becomes a fucking Olympic sport. That's why I have my system: I take pictures. Actual, digital photographs of things I want to verify later.

"I take a lot of pictures of things just to try to keep track, know that this is real or not real," I explained to my latest therapist, who nodded like this was a perfectly reasonable way to navigate existence. "If I can recall it, I call it a... hallucination. If I don't have a picture of it and I'm recalling it, that means... that's not real."

Imagine living like that. Imagine having to create a personal reality verification system just to get through Tuesday.

What's extra hilarious is that my long-term visual memory works flawlessly while my short-term memory is "really, really, really bad." I can recall with perfect clarity what someone wore six months ago but might completely forget my keys sitting right in front of me. It's like my brain has a professional-grade camera but someone keeps stealing the memory cards.

"If you want to remember that jersey, wear this color shirt on this day... I'll remember," I told my therapist. "If we talk about this conversation later in a year or six months... I'll remember."

The catch? "I've learned not to pay attention to things as deeply because then I'll just remember... just too many things and it clogs my brain."

I've had to develop a sophisticated filtering system to prevent mental overload—like having to manually delete spam emails from your brain to keep it functioning. Most people's memories naturally fade and blur. Mine stay crisp and vivid unless I actively work to blur them myself.

But let's talk about the real mindfuck: those childhood memories that you're not sure are real.

Did my dad really give me ketamine by accident when I was an infant? Is that even possible? Did I really see my mom being beaten with a whip while running through that maze-like house with yellow shag carpet and black and white checkered walls? Or did my brain, traumatized and desperate to make sense of something senseless, construct these cinematic memories to explain the inexplicable emotional damage?

Here's a fun fact about trauma and memory: they're both unreliable narrators in the story of your life. Together, they're about as trustworthy as a politician on cocaine.

Then there are the childhood "lies" that seemed so innocent but now feel like premonitions. When I was five, I told kids at school that I owned a hot air balloon and a Mercedes. I carried pictures of them in my pocket as "proof." Pure childhood fantasy, right?

Except years later, I did get to ride in that hot air balloon. It didn't float over my house as I'd claimed, but it was "the people I worked for, but I still got to do it." And I did eventually get the Mercedes. Not the exact one in the picture, but close enough to feel eerily prophetic.

"Everything my brain is fucking with me, in a way, I've done," I realized. "Because I proved myself. Because I told somebody I did it when I was younger, I had to like make sure..."

It's like I've spent my life trying to turn my childhood lies into truths, just to make myself an honest person retroactively. Talk about a long game.

But there's an even more maddening layer to this memory maze: all those things I remember seeing that couldn't possibly be real. The six people following me, taking photos because I was driving too slowly. The tracking software mysteriously appearing on my phone. The shadows that whisper by in my periphery.

Are those hallucinations, or are they memories of hallucinations? And is there even a meaningful difference?

When I told doctors about seeing things that weren't there, they used words like "overactive imagination" and "creative type." When you're a kid who sees things, you're imaginative. When you're an adult who sees things, you're psychotic. Same symptom, different decades, completely different diagnosis.

My childhood "imagination" was likely just early-onset hallucinations with better PR. But the distinction hardly matters when you're five years old and convinced there are monsters under your bed. Whether they're imaginary or hallucinatory, they're still terrifying, and the adults still tell you they're not real.

Except sometimes I wonder if maybe, just maybe, I was seeing things others couldn't. Not in some mystical psychic way—I'm crazy, not delusional (though the distinction sometimes feels academic). But what if some of those "hallucinations" were just my brain processing information others missed? What if my "overactive imagination" was actually my mind desperately trying to fill in gaps in a world that made no sense?

The most dangerous game isn't hunting humans—it's hunting truth in the wilderness of your own mind. Every memory becomes suspect. Every recollection needs corroboration. "Did that happen or did I imagine it?" becomes your internal mantra, muttered like a prayer to a god who's definitely not listening.

So I take pictures. I document. I create external evidence for internal experiences. I build a paper trail of my existence because sometimes I'm not entirely convinced I exist at all. It's exhausting, but it's survival.

And here's the most twisted part of all: despite this elaborate verification system, despite all the pictures and evidence and reality-checking, I still don't know what's real half the time. I'm still walking through life like it's a dream I might wake up from at any moment.

But in the end, does it really matter what's "real" in some objective sense? If I experienced it—whether it was a hallucination, a dream, a memory, or some bizarre hybrid of all three—then it affected me. It shaped me. It became part of my story.

Maybe that's why I'm writing this book. Not just to tell you what happened, but to convince myself that something happened. Whether it's the truth or not, at least it's documented now. At least there's evidence.

And maybe someday, when I look back at these pages, I'll be able to say with certainty: "This, at least, was real. I know because I wrote it down."

Or maybe this entire book is just another elaborate hallucination, and you, dear reader, don't exist at all.

Say cheese for the camera. I need to remember this moment.

It's like having a hinge with half the range of motion, which is exactly as convenient as it sounds.

The ankle was just the beginning. Years later, problems developed in my back because my body compensated for the ankle. I "fake walk really good," but this talent comes at a price: "It's changed the gait of my hips." I've mastered making myself "look like I'm walking normal," but the truth is, I've been in pain since that first injury happened.

You've toured the funhouse mirrors of my perception, walked the tightrope of my daily existence, and witnessed the spectacular circus of dysfunction that is my life. But here's where I'm supposed to deliver the uplifting conclusion, right? The part where I reveal the life-changing epiphany that finally fixed everything. The miracle medication that banished the shadows. The therapeutic breakthrough that healed my childhood wounds. The moment when everything suddenly made sense and I emerged, butterfly-like, from my chrysalis of delusion into a beautiful, sane existence.

Spoiler alert: That's not how this story ends.

Because it doesn't end.

That's the thing about mental illness that all those inspirational recovery memoirs conveniently gloss over. There's no finish line, no moment when you get to spike the football and do a victory dance. There's just... more days. More mornings waking up and playing "Is Today Going to Make Sense?" More shadows in the periphery that may or may not be real. More conversations with people who may or may not exist.

My therapist—assuming she's real (still up for debate)—occasionally asks about my "goals." As if having hallucinatory experiences is something you can set a five-year plan for. "By Q3 of 2023, I hope to reduce shadow people sightings by 15% and increase reality verification efficiency by 22%."

But I do have goals, as absurd as they might seem in the context of a brain that regularly manufactures its own reality. I want to understand my hallucinations better, "why they happen... maybe if I understand them more, they might help me not have them as bad." I want to unpack some childhood trauma, those memories that feel both painfully vivid and suspiciously cinematic. I want to stop stealing things I don't need and immediately throwing them away.

Modest aspirations, I know.

The truth is, living with mental illness isn't about reaching some glorious conclusion where all your symptoms disappear and you join the ranks of the neurotypical. It's about learning to navigate your particular ocean of weirdness without drowning. It's about building a boat sturdy enough to withstand the storms of psychosis, the hurricanes of paranoia, the tsunamis of depression.

My boat is made of routine—"even if it's a bad routine. It's still a routine." Structure is my life raft. When I have it, I can almost pass for normal. When I don't, the shadows get louder, the paranoia more convincing, the impulses harder to resist.

It's made of medication—an ever-evolving cocktail of chemicals trying to correct whatever misfiring synapses make me see things that aren't there and hear suggestions to take things that aren't mine. The pills help, even if they never quite silence everything. They turn the volume down from an unbearable scream to a manageable murmur.

It's made of documentation—photos to verify reality, notes to track symptoms, records of what did or didn't happen. External storage for a memory that can't always be trusted.

It's made of people who anchor me—Harry, who gave me structure before he died; my grandmother, who loved me unconditionally; my mother, who despite her own chaos tries her best; therapists (real or imagined) who listen without judgment.

And now, it's made of this book—this collection of words trying to make sense of senselessness. Writing about my experiences doesn't cure them, but it does something almost as valuable: it transforms them from things that happen to me into things I can examine, understand, and sometimes even find humor in.

So what now? Now I keep going. I wake up each morning and place my bets on reality. I take my pills, follow my routine, document what needs documenting. I try to distinguish between what's real and what's manufactured by my overactive brain. I fail at this regularly, spectacularly, sometimes dangerously—but I keep trying.

And here's where the joke really is on you, dear reader. While you've been comfortably observing my dysfunction from a safe distance, smugly certain of your own grip on reality, I've been leaving breadcrumbs throughout this narrative—little moments of universal doubt, fear, and confusion that made you nod in recognition.

Because the truth is, none of us are as firmly anchored in reality as we like to pretend. Your perception is just as subjective, your memories just as unreliable, your sense of self just as constructed as mine. The difference is merely one of degree, not kind.

You've related to parts of this book that you shouldn't if you were entirely "sane." You've recognized your own paranoid thoughts, your own memory gaps, your own moments of disconnection from consensus reality. You've felt that uncanny sense of "wait, did that really happen or did I imagine it?" at least once in your life.

The line between "mentally ill" and "normal" isn't as clear as our diagnostic manuals would have us believe. It's more like a smudged boundary, a gradient of experience that we all fall somewhere along. Some of us are just further out on the weird end of the spectrum, where the shadows talk and the world occasionally ripples like a disturbed pond.

So if there's any conclusion to be drawn from my ongoing story, maybe it's this: Reality is overrated. Sanity is relative. And we're all just doing our best to make sense of our particular flavor of consciousness with whatever tools we have available.

I'll keep building my boat, patching the holes when they appear, bailing out the water when it gets too deep. I'll keep navigating toward whatever passes for normal in my world. And sometimes, on good days, I might even enjoy the journey—strange sights, unreliable coordinates, and all.

After all, it's my circus. And while there are no refunds, the show is one hell of a ride.

Chapter 13: The Hallucinations Might Have Ended Here (Or Maybe They Just Got Better at Hiding)

I wake up feeling... normal.

That's the first red flag.

The shadows are gone. The voices silent. The paranoia? Nothing but a distant memory, like a fever dream I once had as a child. I stretch, feeling the satisfying pop of joints realigning themselves. My mind is clear. Clearer than it's been in—has it ever been this clear?

I brush my teeth. Normal. I make coffee. Normal. I check my phone. Normal.

Everything is so goddamn normal that it's setting off alarm bells in the parts of my brain that still remember what normal isn't supposed to feel like. For people like me.

It's too quiet. Too clean. Too... scripted.

That's when I notice it. In the bathroom mirror. Just a microsecond delay—my reflection's toothbrush moving a fraction of a second after mine. I blink. My reflection blinks too, but the eyes stay closed just a beat too long.

I step back. It steps back.

"Just the medication," I tell myself. "Just a side effect. We've been through this before."

My phone rings. The number on the screen isn't one I know, which is impossible because I memorize everything. Every number, every address, every face. Every medication dosage and every therapeutic technique and every hallucination I've ever had.

I don't answer. It keeps ringing.

In the kitchen, I open the refrigerator for orange juice and see a plate. Half-eaten steak, congealed fat forming tiny white continents on a sea of bloody juice. I don't eat steak. I haven't eaten steak since I was a kid, standing in that maze of a house with yellow shag carpet and black and white checkered walls.

I close the refrigerator door. I open it again. The steak is still there.

My hand drifts to my pocket, fingers brushing against paper. I pull out a receipt. The date at the top reads three days from now. The items: steak, red wine, sleeping pills. I don't remember buying any of these things. I don't remember going to the store at all.

Is this what healing is supposed to feel like?

Something isn't right. But is it the world that's wrong, or just my perception of it? After decades of hallucinations, maybe this discomfort is just what normalcy feels like to someone who's never really known it.

I step outside. Fresh air. That will help.

My neighbor waves. "Beautiful morning, isn't it? So peaceful today!" His smile is too wide. His teeth too white. Have I ever spoken to this man before? Does he even live here?

A car drives by. Black sedan, license plate 8XTF349. I blink. Another car passes. Black sedan, license plate 8XTF349. I blink harder. A third car. Same make. Same model. Same goddamn license plate.

I look up at the sky. It's blue. But not just any blue—it's perfectly, symmetrically blue. No variation. No clouds. Just an uninterrupted canvas of cerulean that feels more like a poorly rendered video game background than actual atmosphere.

I blink once. I blink twice.

The air changes. Thickens. Like someone's slowly turning up the humidity while simultaneously increasing the air pressure. My ears pop. The world holds its breath.

Then I hear it—static. Faint at first, like a television left on in an empty room. But it's getting louder. Closer. As if someone is dragging a massive electrical appliance across the concrete toward me.

"You're not supposed to notice," a voice whispers, close enough that I feel breath on my ear.

I spin around. Nobody's there. But I'm not taking chances. I run back inside, slamming the door, turning the deadbolt. One lock. Two locks. Three locks—when did I install a third lock?

Check everything. That's what Harry taught me. That's what structure demands. When in doubt, verify.

I grab my journal from the bedside table. Flip it open. The first entry is mine—my handwriting, my words, my memory of a day three months ago when the shadows were particularly active. I turn the page. Still mine. Another page. Still—

Wait.

This entry isn't mine. The handwriting is different—more slanted, the letters pressing harder into the paper. I never press that hard; my hand gets tired too easily. The entry describes a day I have no memory of. A trip to a restaurant I've never been to. A conversation with someone named Marcus. I don't know any Marcus.

Half the journal is filled with these entries. Someone else's life inserted between the pages of mine. Or maybe my life inserted between the pages of someone else's.

I reach for my phone again. Call Sophie—she'll remember. She'll know what's real.

She answers on the second ring.

"Hello?"

It's her voice. Almost. The pitch is slightly higher, the cadence just a beat too slow. Like someone who's studied Sophie extensively but has never quite mastered her exact inflections.

"Sophie? It's me."

"Oh, hey! How are you feeling? Better today?"

"What do you mean, better? I haven't been sick."

A pause. Too long.

"The hospital, remember? Last week? You called me at 3 AM saying the shadows were trying to crawl inside your mouth."

I never called her. I never went to any hospital. The shadows have never tried to—

Have they?

"I need to call you back," I say, and hang up.

Back to the bathroom. I need to see myself. Confirm at least one constant in this shifting nightmare.

The mirror shows me. But not me now—me from ten minutes ago, entering the bathroom for the first time today. I watch as mirror-me brushes my teeth, notices the delay, steps back. Then the reflection catches up to real-time, and I'm staring at myself.

Except I'm not smiling. And my reflection is.

A slow, knowing smirk spreads across its face. It raises one finger to its lips. Shhh.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Three precise taps on the front door. My heart stammers against my ribs like it's trying to break free. I don't move.

"It's me. Open up." A woman's voice. Calm. Professional. Familiar.

Dr. Levine? But that's impossible. Dr. Levine is dead. Or—did I imagine her death? Did I imagine her ?

"I just want to talk," the voice continues. "Aren't you glad we're back?"

We? Who's we?

"You knew we'd never really leave."

My phone buzzes in my hand. A text message from an unknown number:

WE LET YOU REST. THAT'S OVER NOW.

The static is deafening now. It fills the apartment, vibrates through the floorboards, makes my teeth ache and my vision blur. This isn't real. Can't be real. But what if it is? What if this time, finally, I'm seeing what's actually there?

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Louder now. More insistent.

I have two choices:

Open the door. Face whatever's waiting on the other side—something old, something patient, something that's been watching me for decades, wearing different faces, speaking with different voices.

Or run. But where would I go? If the world isn't real anymore—if it never was—then there's nowhere to hide. No structure strong enough to withstand this particular storm.

My feet move toward the door. Not because I want them to, but because I need to know. Because uncertainty has always been more torturous than even the worst confirmation.

My hand reaches for the knob. Turns it. Pulls the door open.

And there stands—

You.

Reading these words. Watching my life unfold like a mildly entertaining Netflix series you put on in the background while scrolling through your phone. Did you think you were just a passive observer? That there was a safe distance between your reality and mine?

That's adorable.

Close this book. Go about your day. Pretend you don't hear that faint static starting at the edges of your awareness. Pretend you don't see that shadow just out of the corner of your eye. Pretend that the boundaries between your mind and mine are solid, impermeable.

But know this: the hallucinations might have ended here. Or maybe they just got better at hiding.

And now they know where you live too.
